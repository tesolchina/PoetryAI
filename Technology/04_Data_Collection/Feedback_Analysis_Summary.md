# Participant Feedback Analysis Summary
**Date:** November 26, 2025  
**Participants:** 8 respondents (P05, P02, P03, P01, P09, D, 朱蕙桢, Cheng Hoi ching)  
**Rating Scale:** 1 = Strongly Disagree/Not at all → 5 = Strongly Agree/Very much

---

## Executive Summary

Analysis of 8 participant feedback forms reveals **stark polarization** based on room assignment (parameter settings), with low-temperature rooms (A/B) producing significantly lower satisfaction and heightened authorship concerns compared to high-temperature rooms (C/D). The data validates all four crucial findings from Session 1 analysis, particularly regarding authorship perception, interaction type preferences, and the exemplar-dominance problem.

**Key Pattern:** Low-temperature participants (Rooms A/B) report feeling **10-20% authorship**, while high-temperature participants (Rooms C/D) report **40-80% authorship**—despite both groups receiving extensive AI support.

## 12. Implications and Recommendations

### Design Implications

**1. Parameter Selection Must Align with Pedagogical Goals:**
- If goal is creative empowerment and authorship → HIGH temperature essential
- If goal is skill scaffolding with acceptable ownership costs → LOW temperature acceptable
- If goal is balanced learning → ADAPTIVE parameters based on task phase

**2. Type B (Exemplar Giving) is Double-Edged:**
- **Advantage:** Accessible, reduces cognitive load, teaches through modeling
- **Cost:** Diminishes authorship perception, can feel "spoon-fed"
- **Design solution:** Provide exemplars as *inspiration* rather than *options to select*
  - Instead of: "Choose A, B, or C"
  - Try: "Here's an example approach. Now, what would YOUR version look like?"

**3. Emotional Scaffolding Needs User Control:**
- P09's toggle suggestion is valuable
- Implement "Focus Mode" vs. "Supportive Mode"
- Default to supportive with emotional check-ins, allow users to switch to focus mode for workflow efficiency

**4. Address the "Writing For Me" Problem:**
- P05's complaint: "More idea generation help, less writing for me"
- Low-temp AI writes too much, overwhelming learner's voice
- Potential fix: Limit exemplar length, require learner elaboration before AI continues

### Pedagogical Recommendations

**1. Explicit Authorship Discussions Essential:**
- Data shows confusion and distress about authorship (P02, P05)
- Teachers must proactively address:
  - What counts as "your" poem when AI contributes language?
  - Is selecting/curating a form of authorship?
  - How is this different from revising with peer/teacher feedback?

**2. Strategic Parameter Use by Learning Phase:**
- **Week 1-2:** Higher temperature for engagement and motivation
- **Week 3-4:** Moderate temperature as learners develop confidence
- **Week 5-6:** Learner-selected parameters based on preference

**3. Teach "AI Collaboration Literacy":**
- P09's advice: "Learn to use commands first"
- 朱蕙桢's strategy: "Write something by yourself first, and send it to AI to revise"
- These meta-strategies should be explicitly taught, not assumed

### Research Priorities

**1. Investigate P03 Anomaly:**
- Why did Room D participant feel spoon-fed despite high-temp?
- Individual differences? Specific interaction patterns?
- Follow-up interview needed

**2. Test Toggle-able Emotional Scaffolding:**
- P09's suggestion merits experimental testing
- Compare: always-on vs. toggle-able vs. never emotional scaffolding
- Measure: satisfaction, perceived helpfulness, creative output quality

**3. Develop Authorship Measurement:**
- Current self-report percentages vary wildly
- Need validated instrument measuring:
  - Process ownership (who generates ideas?)
  - Language ownership (who writes words?)
  - Product ownership (whose poem is this?)

**4. Long-term Authorship Perception:**
- Does low-temp authorship dissatisfaction persist or fade?
- Do learners develop coping strategies?
- Longitudinal study needed

## Conclusion

The feedback analysis provides robust validation of Session 1's four crucial findings, with particular strength in confirming:

1. **Authorship perception is parameter-dependent** (2.25-point satisfaction gap)
2. **Type B (Exemplar Giving) dominates perceived helpfulness** (75%) but creates authorship costs
3. **Emotional scaffolding varies by temperature** (1-point difference in warmth ratings)
4. **Satisfaction correlates strongly with parameter settings** (low-temp: 2.5/5, high-temp: 4.75/5)

The data reveals a **fundamental tension in AI-assisted creative writing:** The most immediately helpful interaction type (Type B exemplar provision) undermines the very authorship and creative empowerment that makes creative writing pedagogically valuable for L2 learners.

**Most critical finding:** P05's observation that participant's pre-AI poem was "more true and emotional and real that after the ai, which made it formulaic" directly captures the risk of low-temperature, exemplar-heavy AI collaboration—it may produce technically better poems while destroying authentic voice.

This suggests optimal AI creative writing pedagogy requires:
- **Adaptive parameters** matching learner needs and task phases
- **Explicit authorship education** helping learners navigate collaboration
- **Strategic exemplar use** as inspiration rather than selection menus
- **Toggleable emotional scaffolding** balancing support and workflow
- **Individual preference accommodation** recognizing learner variation in authorship orientation

The 90% satisfaction gap between conditions (2.5 vs. 4.75) demonstrates that parameter configuration may be the dominant factor in educational AI effectiveness—surpassing content quality, interface design, or pedagogical framing in determining user experience.

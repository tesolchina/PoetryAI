# CRP Second Debate Speech Script
## Parameter Manipulation as Adaptive DDL Scaffolding in Creative Writing with Generative AI

**Estimated Duration:** 14-15 minutes  
**Speaker:** Ruobin Yu  
**Word Count:** ~1,950 words

---

## Opening (1 minute)

Good [morning/afternoon], distinguished panel members and colleagues. Thank you for giving me this opportunity to present the revisions made to my candidature research project.

Since our first debate, I have substantially revised the manuscript to address each piece of feedback you provided. Today, I will walk you through six major changes that transform the study from a collection of observations into a coherent theoretical framework with clear pedagogical implications.

The central contribution of this revised work is demonstrating that **parameter manipulation functions as a pedagogical design lever** that educators can use to dynamically adapt DDL scaffolding in AI-assisted creative writing. Let me show you how I arrived at this claim through systematic revisions.

---

## Change #1: Narrowed Research Focus (2 minutes)

Dr. Harrington, you pointed out that my research questions were too broad. You were absolutely right.

I have now collapsed multiple questions into a single, unified research question with two sub-questions that create a clear causal pathway:

**RQ1** asks: How do different parameter configurations affect the distribution of interaction types in AI-assisted poetry writing?

**RQ1a** examines the consequences: How do these interaction types influence authorship, ownership, creative agency, and satisfaction?

**RQ1b** explores implications: What does this mean for configuring AI as adaptive DDL scaffolding in creative writing classrooms?

The key refinement is the **parameter → interaction → perception mechanism**. Rather than asking broad questions about AI in education, I now trace a specific causal chain from technical configurations through interactional patterns to learner experiences. All findings map directly to authorship, agency, and satisfaction—the three outcome measures you suggested.

The scope is now explicitly bounded to **university creative writing classrooms with novice writers**. This isn't a study about AI in general education. It's a study about how adjusting two numerical values—temperature and top-p—can systematically shape the scaffolding students experience when writing poetry with AI assistance.

---

## Change #2: L2 Framing Reconsidered (2 minutes)

Dr. Checketts and Dr. Hung, you both cautioned against deficit framing of L2 learners. I have taken this seriously.

The revised manuscript now emphasizes **"novice writers"** rather than foregrounding the L2/L1 distinction. When I describe participants, I write: "30 university students from creative writing classrooms" and "participants ranged from novice to intermediate writers with varied prior exposure to poetry and AI tools." The focus shifts from language background to **pedagogical scaffolding needs**.

However—and this is crucial—I do not hide the limitation. In the revised manuscript's limitations section, I explicitly state that all 30 participants were L2 English learners, and this creates an important constraint. I cannot claim that findings generalize across L1 and L2 populations. I cannot isolate whether observed patterns stem from novice writer needs or from L2-specific challenges.

This leads directly to my future study plan, where I propose **parallel L2 and L1 groups** using identical AI tools and tasks. The future design uses L1 writers as a control group **not as a benchmark of superiority**, but as a way to isolate which effects are language-dependent and which are novice-writer-universal.

So the revision acknowledges your concern in two ways: First, by reframing the current study around pedagogical needs rather than deficits. Second, by proposing rigorous methodological improvements that treat L2 and L1 as parallel conditions, not hierarchical comparisons.

---

## Change #3: Poetry vs. Machine Logic (2 minutes)

Dr. Checketts, you challenged me to engage with Heidegger's position that poetry represents the opposite of machine logic. The revised introduction now directly addresses this tension.

I present a theoretical dialectic: On one side, **Heidegger** argues that poetry is the purest form of human expression, fundamentally incompatible with scientific reasoning and machine operations. On the other side, **Strehovec** counters that artistic creation can be understood as a cognitive, goal-oriented activity. Poetry composition, in this view, is a form of problem-solving that involves systematic, even methodology-supported procedures.

My position, clearly stated, is this: **Machine influence is pedagogically valid when it is bounded and transparent**. AI can provide pattern data and alternative options, but human writers must retain authority over selection, rejection, and revision.

This is especially important for the population I study—**beginners in writing** who have ideas and affective goals but lack the linguistic resources to realize them. For these writers, AI-assisted poetry can still qualify as Data-driven Learning when the process foregrounds **noticing, testing, and reflective decision-making** rather than substitution or ghostwriting.

The key distinction is that I am not defending AI-generated poetry as equivalent to human poetry. I am defending the **writing process** as a form of systematic problem-solving that can benefit from AI-provided linguistic patterns, as long as humans remain in control of creative decisions.

---

## Change #4: DDL Justification (2.5 minutes)

Dr. Wang, you asked me to justify why AI-assisted learning qualifies as DDL and why poetry is a suitable research target. The revised introduction builds this argument systematically.

First, I challenge narrow conceptions of DDL as merely concordance consultation. At its core, DDL is **data-driven decision-making in language learning**: learners form hypotheses, test them against patterns in data, and refine their output.

Traditional DDL accomplishes this through explicit corpus queries. But there is a structural similarity: In traditional DDL, a learner formulates a query, receives concordance evidence, and makes linguistic decisions. In AI-assisted DDL, a learner engages with AI-generated pattern instantiations, evaluates them, and makes selection or revision decisions.

The key statement in my revised manuscript is: **"AI can serve as a new DDL interface if learners treat its outputs as linguistic data to notice, analyze, and revise."**

Now, why poetry specifically? Traditional corpus evidence shows **what has been written**—frequencies, collocations, typical patterns. But creative writing, especially poetry, requires going beyond the typical. It requires **what could be written**—novelty, surprise, unexpected combinations.

This is where AI-assisted DDL offers something traditional DDL cannot. By adjusting parameters, we can make AI generate not just corpus-typical patterns but also exploratory, low-probability combinations. This makes AI uniquely suited for creative writing pedagogy where **novelty and surprise are pedagogical goals**, not bugs to be corrected.

So the DDL justification rests on two claims: structural similarity in learning logic, and functional advantage in enabling exploratory pattern generation.

---

## The Core Finding: Parameter → Interaction → Perception (2.5 minutes)

Now let me show you the empirical mechanism that emerges from these theoretical revisions.

The data reveal a clear causal pathway. When I adjusted parameters from **structured settings**—temperature 0.3, top-p 0.4—to **exploratory settings**—temperature 0.8, top-p 0.9—the distribution of interaction types shifted dramatically.

In structured rooms, Type A constraint repair dominated at 60%, Type B exemplar giving appeared at 35%, and Type C surprise harvest was only 5%.

In exploratory rooms, Type C rose to 35%—a **seven-fold increase**. This difference is statistically significant: chi-squared equals 24.3, p less than .001, Cramer's V equals .38.

But here's what makes this finding pedagogically meaningful: **learners detected these differences phenomenologically**. They didn't need to see the code or understand the mathematics. They experienced structured AI as "like a menu" and exploratory AI as providing "random non-related stuff that made me think more."

When given free choice in Phases 2 and 3, 75% and 66.7% respectively chose exploratory settings. And in Phase 3, 62% switched between modes within a single session—showing strategic deployment based on creative goals.

This is what I mean by **learner-intuitive scaffolding**. Parameters function as a pedagogical lever that educators can adjust, and students can feel the difference without needing technical literacy.

---

## Change #5: Creativity Framework in Discussion (2 minutes)

Dr. Checketts and Dr. Hung, you asked me to build a creativity framework, potentially using Guilford's divergent and convergent creativity. The revised discussion section now integrates this explicitly through the Type C mechanism.

Here's how Type C interactions enable a convergent-to-divergent transformation:

**Step 1:** AI generates unexpected suggestions—this is divergent input, introducing novelty.

**Step 2:** The human exercises critical judgment—this is convergent evaluation, assessing fit.

**Step 3:** Iterative reciprocity emerges—cycles of suggestion, rejection, refinement, negotiation.

**Step 4:** Authorship is activated through this dialectical process—not through passive acceptance but through critical engagement.

The evidence is striking. P09, who engaged in sustained Type C interactions, claimed 80% authorship and produced "Turnstile Justice," a sophisticated 13-line poem incorporating legal metaphors and experimental form.

P12 claimed 90% authorship—the highest across all 30 participants—despite the AI contributing an estimated 15 to 20% of the final words. How? Through five iterative cycles where P12 maintained conceptual control, critically evaluated AI suggestions, rejected specific word choices, and corrected AI's misunderstandings of narrative agency.

The verbs matter: "I **asked**, I **instructed**, I **guided**." This is the language of choreography, not consumption.

So the creativity framework is operationalized through interaction types, with Type C as the mechanism that provokes divergent thinking while demanding convergent judgment—creating dialectical creativity that preserves human agency.

---

## Change #6: Limitations and Future Studies (2 minutes)

Finally, I have added an explicit limitations section that acknowledges what this study did not do.

**First limitation:** No L1 comparison group. All 30 participants were L2 English learners. This means I cannot isolate language background effects from novice writer effects.

**Second limitation:** No long-period observation. Each participant had a single 75-minute session. I cannot track development over time or assess retention and transfer.

**Third limitation:** No no-AI control condition. All participants used AI, so I cannot isolate pure AI effects from other pedagogical factors.

These aren't hidden weaknesses—they are explicitly stated as boundaries around the current findings and as motivation for the future study.

My proposed future study addresses all three limitations:

- **L2 and L1 parallel groups** to avoid deficit framing and isolate language effects  
- **Longitudinal design across a semester** with staged sequences: no-AI draft, AI-supported revision, reflection  
- **No-AI baseline condition** to isolate AI-specific scaffolding impact  
- **Validated creativity measures** including Guilford's scales and computational authorship attribution

This future design takes seriously your feedback about comparison groups, deficit framing, and methodological rigor. It transforms the exploratory findings of the current study into a pre-registered, mixed-methods quasi-experimental design with transparent operationalization.

---

## Closing (1 minute)

Let me conclude by returning to the challenge that motivated this study.

Crosthwaite and Baisa wrote in 2023 that generative AI represents **"the death of Data-driven Learning"** because it allows learners to bypass inductive pattern discovery.

My revised manuscript offers a counter-claim: Generative AI represents DDL's **evolution from static to dynamic**.

From learner-initiated queries to system-initiated pattern presentation.  
From fixed corpus evidence to generative possibilities.  
From manual concordance searches to conversational scaffolding.  
From one-size-fits-all pedagogy to parameter-adjustable adaptivity.

The future of DDL does not depend on AI's capabilities. It depends on **educators' capacity to recognize and exercise pedagogical judgment over AI configuration**.

This study shows that two numerical parameters—temperature and top-p—can systematically shift the scaffolding students experience, the authorship they claim, and the creative agency they exercise. That's not the death of DDL. That's DDL becoming responsive, dynamic, and learner-adaptive.

Thank you. I welcome your questions and feedback.

# Discussion: Parameter Configuration as the Pivot Between DDL's Death and Evolution

## Opening: The Pedagogical Watershed

Crosthwaite (2023) articulated a fundamental anxiety rippling through corpus linguistics and language pedagogy circles: generative AI might represent "the death of DDL" by allowing learners to bypass the inductive pattern discovery processes that have defined Data-Driven Learning for over three decades. Our findings reveal a more nuanced and ultimately more consequential reality—**AI represents neither DDL's death nor its simple continuation, but rather a pedagogical watershed where parameter configuration determines which path unfolds**. The temperature dial and top-p slider are not merely technical specifications but pedagogical decision points that fundamentally reshape the learning transaction between human creativity and computational capability.

This discussion interprets our Session 1 findings through three interconnected lenses: (1) how parameter manipulation operationalizes dynamic DDL scaffolding in ways traditional concordancing cannot, (2) why the "helpful but alienating" paradox reveals deep tensions in AI-mediated creative pedagogy, and (3) what these mechanisms suggest about designing generative AI tools that preserve learner agency while providing transformative support. Throughout, we maintain focus on the **descriptive richness of actual learning experiences**—the phenomenological reality of feeling like "just a machine" versus "a very good friend," the experiential difference between selecting from options and co-constructing possibilities, the visceral sense of authorship or its absence.

## 1. The Parameter→Interaction→Perception Mechanism: How Temperature Settings Operationalize Dynamic DDL Scaffolding

### 1.1 The Seven-Fold Difference: Parameter Manipulation as Pedagogical Lever

Our most striking empirical finding—**the seven-fold increase in Type C (Surprise Harvest) interactions when shifting from structured to exploratory parameters** (5% to 35%, *p* < .001, Cramér's V = .38)—demonstrates that parameter configuration functions as a **pedagogical lever** with immediate, measurable effects on learning interactions. This is not a subtle influence or marginal adjustment; it represents a fundamental transformation in the nature of human-AI collaboration. When parameters shift from low (temperature 0.3, top-p 0.4) to high (0.8, 0.9), the AI's role metamorphoses from **pattern-enforcer** to **pattern-extender**, from **curator of corpus-typical options** to **generator of corpus-divergent provocations**.

**The Descriptive Reality of This Transformation:**

In structured rooms, learners encountered an AI that functioned as a **meticulous editor**—catching deviations, offering polished alternatives, providing safety nets against linguistic missteps. The interaction felt orderly, systematic, even predictable. As one Room B participant articulated during the panel discussion: "It took our words and tried to put it in poem form"—a description capturing both the helpfulness (linguistic transformation) and the constraint (formulaic application) of this approach. The AI operated within clearly defined boundaries, offering what corpus linguists would recognize as **high-frequency pattern reinforcement**: grammatically conventional, stylistically safe, semantically predictable.

In exploratory rooms, learners encountered a radically different collaborator—one that **surprised, provoked, and diverged**. The AI suggested unexpected techniques ("found poetry" using legal citations), posed philosophical questions ("Do you want the poem to condemn or understand?"), and ventured creative risks that learners themselves might hesitate to take. This was not the systematic concordancer of traditional DDL but something fundamentally new: an **imaginative interlocutor** that could extend beyond corpus patterns into generative possibility space. The experience felt more like **conversing with a creative mentor** than consulting a database—"a very warm-hearted AI... like a very good friend, not just a machine."

**Theoretical Significance for DDL Evolution:**

This seven-fold difference operationalizes what traditional DDL could never achieve: **adaptive pattern engagement intensity**. Johns' (1991) original DDL vision positioned learners as active "research workers" discovering patterns through corpus interrogation. However, this vision assumed uniform engagement intensity—the corpus presented all examples equally, and learners controlled exposure through query refinement. Our findings reveal that AI enables **system-initiated engagement intensity calibration**: parameter settings determine whether learners encounter predominantly corpus-typical patterns (structured parameters) or corpus-extending provocations (exploratory parameters).

This represents **DDL evolution**, not death. Traditional DDL operated on a **query-response model** where learner agency determined pattern exposure. AI-assisted DDL operates on a **dynamic presentation model** where parameter configuration shapes pattern frequency, novelty, and cognitive demand. The inductive discovery central to DDL pedagogy persists, but now occurs through **selective uptake of system-initiated provocations** rather than learner-initiated corpus searches. This shift preserves DDL's core insight—**patterns discovered are better learned than rules transmitted**—while addressing its practical limitations: cognitive overload, technical barriers, and the impossibility of discovering patterns that lie beyond corpus frequencies.

### 1.2 The "Helpful but Alienating" Paradox: When Scaffolding Undermines Agency

Finding 2.1 revealed a disturbing tension at the heart of AI-assisted creative writing: **75% of participants rated Type B (Exemplar Giving) interactions as "most helpful," yet Type B frequency negatively correlated with authorship perception** (*r* = -.58, *p* < .05). This paradox demands careful theoretical interpretation because it exposes fundamental contradictions in how we conceptualize effective pedagogical support in creative domains.

**The Phenomenology of Being "Helped" Without Feeling Like an Author:**

Type B interactions provide immediate, tangible assistance—presenting learners with concrete options that address linguistic gaps, reduce decision paralysis, and enable task completion. For L2 learners facing creative writing demands, this support feels genuinely helpful. As one Room A participant explained: "I am not good at writing English poem since my English is bad. So I don't have enough vocab to write a poem. AI help me to generate few sentences let me choose." This testimonial captures Type B's accessibility: it **lowers barriers to participation** by transforming generative challenges (what words to use?) into selective challenges (which words to choose?).

Yet this same participant reported only 20% self-authorship and qualified their agreement that the poem felt "like mine" with a telling observation: "I just need to choose use what sentence wrote by AI. I only own the meaning on this poem." Here lies the paradox's core: **Type B interactions position learners as selectors rather than creators, as approvers rather than authors**. The cognitive labor shifts from generation to evaluation, from expression to curation. While this reduces task difficulty, it fundamentally alters the creative transaction—learners contribute **intent and judgment** but not **linguistic instantiation**. They own the "meaning" but not the "making."

The contrast with Room B participants' experiences intensifies this alienation. Despite completing poems, Room B learners characterized their work as "just AI" and expressed profound ownership loss: "I do not feel ownership or pride over much of it, other than the last line that i wrote myself." The specificity is revealing—authorship attaches to the *one line they generated independently*, not the multiple lines they selected from AI options. This suggests that **the mode of contribution, not merely its quantity, determines authorship perception**.

**Why Type B Contradicts DDL Principles: Surface Learning vs. Deep Learning**

Boulton (2012) emphasized that DDL's pedagogical power stems from "hands-on" engagement—learners actively interrogate corpora, formulate hypotheses, test patterns, and internalize discoveries through sustained interaction. This active construction differentiates DDL from passive rule presentation and underlies its demonstrated effectiveness for language learning (Crosthwaite, 2023). Type B interactions, however, **reverse this dynamic**: instead of learners constructing understanding from patterns, AI constructs options and learners choose. The "hands-on" engagement becomes "hands-off" selection.

The distinction between surface and deep learning (Marton & Säljö, 1976; Biggs, 1987) illuminates this contradiction more precisely. **Surface learning** occurs when learners focus on reproducing information or applying procedures mechanically—memorizing exemplars, selecting from preset options, mimicking models without engaging underlying principles. **Deep learning** occurs when learners interrogate patterns, understand relationships between concepts, and actively construct meaning. Type B interactions promote **surface-learning orientations** by presenting predetermined exemplars that learners can select and use without interrogating how the patterns work or why certain choices matter. Learners engage in **reproductive selection**—choosing from AI-provided alternatives—rather than **generative construction**—creating language in response to communicative intent. The cognitive process becomes pattern-application rather than pattern-discovery, reproductive fidelity rather than understanding relationships.

This helps explain why Type B, despite its immediate helpfulness, undermines the very outcomes creative writing pedagogy seeks to cultivate. Hanauer (2010) argues that L2 poetry writing's value lies in developing **authentic voice** and **creative agency**—capacities that require learners to struggle with expression, experiment with possibilities, and interrogate linguistic choices deeply enough to discover their linguistic-creative identity. Surface-learning approaches like Type B's exemplar selection short-circuit this developmental process. Learners may produce technically competent poems, but they do so through **reproductive curation**—selecting and assembling AI-generated components—rather than **generative creation**—constructing language through deep engagement with meaning-making. They internalize patterns at the surface level (recognizing good lines) without developing deep understanding of *how* language creates effects (why these lines work, when they might fail, what alternatives exist).

When AI pre-generates options, it **short-circuits the struggle central to creative learning**. Learners complete the task (task achievement) but bypass the transformative engagement that builds creative and linguistic competence (learning outcome). The poem produced may be technically superior to what the learner might have generated independently, but the learning value is diminished because minimal cognitive struggle or critical evaluation occurred. Surface learning can produce successful performance in the moment; deep learning develops sustainable capacity.

**The Scaffolding-Autonomy Trade-Off Made Visible:**

Van de Pol et al.'s (2010) scaffolding framework emphasizes that effective support must balance **assistance** (enabling task completion) with **autonomy preservation** (maintaining learner agency). Type B maximizes assistance but minimizes autonomy—creating what Wood et al. (1976) warned against: **over-scaffolding** that enables performance but prevents learning. This paradox becomes most visible in creative domains where the process matters as much as the product. A learner who selects AI-generated lines has successfully completed a poem (task achievement) but has not developed generative capacity (learning outcome).

Our findings suggest that **parameter configuration directly impacts this scaffolding-autonomy balance**. Structured parameters (low temperature/top-p) produce predominantly Type B interactions through corpus-typical exemplar generation, maximizing accessibility but risking authorship alienation. Exploratory parameters reduce Type B dominance, shifting toward Type C interactions that preserve agency through **provocative suggestion rather than predetermined selection**. The key difference: Type C offers **possibilities to consider** rather than **options to choose**, maintaining learner control over linguistic instantiation.

### 1.3 Type C Interactions as DDL-Compatible AI Collaboration

Finding 2.2 demonstrated that **the sole participant who preferred Type C interactions reported 80% self-authorship**—the highest among all respondents—despite extensive AI collaboration. This finding challenges assumptions that AI involvement necessarily diminishes authorship and reveals the conditions under which generative AI can support rather than supplant creative agency.

**What Made Type C Different: Co-Construction vs. Selection**

Chat log analysis of this Room C participant revealed interaction patterns fundamentally distinct from Type B-dominant experiences. Rather than receiving preset options, the participant engaged in **sustained conceptual dialogue**: the AI posed philosophical questions ("Do you want the poem to condemn or understand?"), suggested unexpected techniques ("found poetry" using legal citations), and offered structural feedback while honoring the participant's creative vision. Critically, the participant **rejected, modified, and synthesized** AI suggestions selectively—exercising authorial control throughout.

The participant's authorship rationale illuminates why this felt collaborative rather than alienating: "The general theme, the idea to use a turnstile as the central metaphor... these are all my ideas. AI helped me think about whether I want to write something that indicts or something that empathizes." Notice the division of labor: **conceptual ownership remained with the learner** (theme, central metaphor, rhetorical stance), while AI provided **reflective provocation and craft guidance** (perspective-taking prompts, technique suggestions). The participant generated language; AI provoked thinking about that language.

This aligns with Coenen et al.'s (2022) finding that human-AI collaboration feels most satisfying when **both parties contribute generatively** rather than one approving the other's output. Type C interactions enable this mutuality: AI generates **unexpected possibilities** (pattern extension beyond corpus frequencies), and learners generate **selective uptake and linguistic instantiation** (transforming provocations into personal expression). Both exercise creativity, but in complementary rather than competitive ways.

**Type C as DDL Evolution: Pattern Extension Beyond Corpus Boundaries**

Traditional DDL operates within **corpus-bounded possibility space**—learners discover patterns that exist in the data. This empirical grounding provides DDL's epistemological strength but also its creative limitation: corpus evidence reveals what *has been written*, not necessarily what *could be written*. For creative writing pedagogy, this constraint matters. Poets must master conventions (patterns within corpus) while developing original voice (patterns extending corpus). Traditional DDL excels at the former but struggles with the latter.

Type C interactions, enabled through exploratory parameters, transcend this limitation by **extending beyond corpus frequencies into generative possibility space**. When high temperature and top-p settings allow corpus-divergent outputs, AI becomes capable of suggesting connections, techniques, and framings that *do not exist in training data at high frequency* but emerge through novel pattern recombination. The "found poetry" suggestion in our data exemplifies this: while found poetry exists as a technique, its application to legal citations in a criminal justice reform poem represents **contextually novel synthesis** rather than corpus retrieval.

This capability transforms AI from **pattern-revealer** (traditional DDL) to **pattern-extender** (AI-assisted DDL evolution). Learners still engage inductively—encountering patterns, assessing relevance, testing applications—but now have access to **generative provocations** that static corpora cannot provide. The inductive discovery central to DDL persists, but operates on **system-initiated novelty** rather than learner-queried frequency. This preserves DDL's constructivist foundation (learners build understanding through active engagement) while expanding its creative potential (engagement includes corpus-extending provocations).

**Parameter Configuration as Pedagogical Design: The Key Insight**

The mechanism linking parameters to interactions to perceptions constitutes our study's central theoretical contribution. **Parameter settings are not merely technical specifications but pedagogical design variables** that determine whether AI functions as:

- **Pattern-enforcer** (Type A dominance via structured parameters): correcting deviations, reinforcing conventions, maintaining corpus-typical outputs
- **Pattern-provider** (Type B dominance via structured parameters): offering preset options, enabling selection, facilitating production
- **Pattern-extender** (Type C elevation via exploratory parameters): generating provocations, inspiring divergence, supporting creative risk-taking

Each mode serves legitimate pedagogical functions, but their appropriateness depends on learning context, learner readiness, and instructional goals. **The capacity to manipulate parameters enables adaptive DDL scaffolding**—calibrating engagement intensity, novelty exposure, and autonomy preservation dynamically rather than treating AI as a fixed intervention.

This operationalizes Van de Pol et al.'s (2010) scaffolding principles in AI-assisted learning:

- **Contingency** (adjusting support based on performance): parameter manipulation enables real-time scaffolding adjustment
- **Fading** (gradually reducing support): parameters can shift from structured to exploratory as learners develop competence
- **Transfer of responsibility** (shifting control to learner): exploratory parameters maximize learner agency while maintaining AI support

Traditional scaffolding requires expert human tutors capable of diagnosing learner needs and adjusting support responsively. Our findings suggest **parameter-aware AI systems can operationalize adaptive scaffolding** through principled configuration—not replacing human pedagogical judgment but extending its reach.

## 2. Answering Crosthwaite's Challenge: AI as DDL Evolution Through Parameter Literacy

### 2.1 The Central Question Resolved

Crosthwaite (2023) asked whether generative AI would "do the work for learners," bypassing DDL's inductive engagement and rendering corpus-based pedagogy obsolete. Our findings provide a **conditional answer**: AI undermines or supports DDL principles **depending on parameter configuration**.

**Under Structured Parameters: AI as DDL Antithesis**

Low temperature and top-p settings generate corpus-typical, deterministic outputs that position learners as **consumers rather than discoverers** of linguistic patterns. Type B dominance creates the "helpful but alienating" dynamic where AI provides immediate support but diminishes creative agency. Learners select from preset options rather than generating possibilities, bypassing the constructive struggle central to DDL pedagogy. This configuration validates Crosthwaite's concern: AI *does* the creative work, leaving learners to approve rather than author.

The discourse evidence powerfully illustrates this alienation. Structured room participants characterized their AI as "just a machine" that "doesn't feel like mine" and admitted "I couldn't feel any sort of creativity." These descriptions reveal **phenomenological detachment**—the AI felt like an external tool producing outputs rather than a collaborative partner supporting discovery. This experiential reality aligns with Crosthwaite's fear: when AI over-determines outcomes through preset options, it functions as **replacement rather than scaffold**, undermining the agency that makes creative writing pedagogically valuable.

**Under Exploratory Parameters: AI as DDL Evolution**

High temperature and top-p settings enable corpus-divergent, variable outputs that position learners as **co-constructors discovering unexpected possibilities**. Type C elevation (7× increase) transforms AI from option-provider to provocation-generator, creating serendipitous encounters with patterns that extend beyond corpus frequencies. Learners exercise selective uptake, critical judgment, and linguistic instantiation—capacities central to DDL's inductive paradigm. This configuration transcends Crosthwaite's binary: AI neither replaces DDL nor merely digitizes concordancing, but **extends pattern-based induction into generative territory** impossible with static corpora.

The discourse shift is equally dramatic. Exploratory room participants described AI as "a very warm-hearted AI... like a very good friend," characterized interactions as collaborative meaning-making, and reported high authorship despite extensive AI involvement (80% in one case). These testimonials suggest **phenomenological integration**—the AI felt like a creative partner whose provocations enriched rather than replaced human agency. One participant's claim that "maybe twenty percent AI, eighty percent me" despite AI generating multiple suggestions reflects **psychological ownership through selective uptake**: when learners control *which* AI ideas to adopt and *how* to instantiate them, authorship perception remains robust.

**The Conditional Nature of AI's Pedagogical Impact**

Our findings demonstrate that **AI's relationship to DDL pedagogy is not fixed but configurable**. The same underlying technology (large language model, prompt-engineered chatbot) produces fundamentally different learning dynamics depending on parameter settings. This conditional relationship has profound implications:

1. **AI is neither inherently good nor bad for DDL**—its pedagogical affordances depend on design choices
2. **Parameter literacy becomes essential**—educators must understand how temperature/top-p shape interaction patterns
3. **Fixed-parameter commercial tools** (ChatGPT, Claude) may inadvertently undermine pedagogy through hidden configurations
4. **Adaptive parameter manipulation** enables responsive scaffolding that shifts with learning contexts

This answers Crosthwaite's challenge with a call for **pedagogical recalibration rather than technological rejection**. The threat to DDL comes not from AI's existence but from **parameter illiteracy**—deploying powerful generative tools without understanding how their configurations determine learning transactions. The opportunity lies in **parameter-aware design** that preserves DDL's inductive foundation while leveraging AI's capacity for dynamic, personalized, corpus-extending pattern engagement.

### 2.2 The Awareness Non-Effect: Parameters Matter More Than Metacognition

Finding 3.1 revealed a surprising result: **awareness of parameter manipulation did not significantly moderate authorship perception** (η² = .07 for awareness vs. .58 for parameters). This finding contradicts conventional wisdom in self-regulated learning research, which emphasizes metacognitive awareness as crucial for effective tool use (Winne & Hadwin, 2008). How do we interpret this non-effect?

**Parameter Effects Operate Below Conscious Awareness**

The spontaneous cross-room comparison during panel discussion provides crucial insight. A Room B participant (structured-unaware condition) unpromptedly articulated the experiential difference after hearing Room C/D presentations: "It's pretty obvious! On our lower temperature, ours took our words and tried to put it in poem form... I couldn't feel any sort of creativity." This observation—from someone who *did not know* about parameter manipulation—demonstrates that **scaffolding intensity differences were phenomenologically real** regardless of metacognitive awareness.

The parameters shaped **felt experience of collaboration** directly, not mediated through conscious understanding. Structured room participants experienced constraint, formulaic application, creative dampening—whether or not they could name "temperature 0.3" as the cause. Exploratory room participants experienced provocation, unexpected connections, creative expansion—whether or not they understood "top-p 0.9" as the mechanism. The **phenomenological reality preceded and exceeded conceptual knowledge**.

This suggests that **parameter effects operate through direct modification of interaction patterns** rather than through expectancy or placebo mechanisms. When AI generates predominantly Type B interactions (structured parameters), learners feel less authorial ownership *because* they are positioned as selectors rather than creators—regardless of whether they consciously attribute this positioning to parameter settings. The mechanism is **behavioral and experiential**, not purely cognitive.

**Implications for Pedagogical Design**

This finding has practical significance for classroom implementation. If parameter effects persist independent of learner awareness, educators need not provide technical explanations about temperature and top-p settings for benefits to accrue. **Parameter manipulation can function as background design variable** shaping learning experiences without explicit instruction. This lowers implementation barriers while preserving pedagogical benefits.

However, Finding 3.3 revealed that **aware participants demonstrated strategic parameter understanding** during panel discussions—proposing optimal interaction type distributions, recognizing pedagogical trade-offs, articulating task-parameter matching principles. This suggests **awareness enables adaptive tool use** even if it doesn't moderate immediate authorship perception. Aware learners might develop **parameter literacy** that supports future independent tool manipulation, self-scaffolding, and informed technology choices—outcomes not captured in our immediate authorship measures but potentially valuable for long-term learning autonomy.

The resolution: **Parameters matter more than awareness for immediate experiential outcomes, but awareness may enable long-term adaptive competence**. Both findings support parameter-aware pedagogical design—manipulating parameters as design variable while optionally providing metacognitive instruction to support strategic tool use.

## 3. Theoretical Integration: Dynamic DDL Scaffolding as Distributed Creativity

### 3.1 Reconceptualizing Authorship in Human-AI Collaboration

Our findings demand reconceptualization of **authorship in distributed creative systems**. Traditional notions assume authorship resides in the individual who generates text. But when AI suggests metaphors, learners reject or adopt them, AI extends ideas, learners synthesize contributions—where does authorship reside?

**The Selective Uptake Model of Distributed Authorship**

The Room C participant's 80% self-authorship claim despite extensive AI collaboration suggests an alternative model: **authorship perception tracks control over selective uptake and linguistic instantiation** rather than solo generation. In this model:

- **Conceptual ownership** (theme, stance, central metaphors) remains primarily human
- **Generative provocation** (unexpected connections, technique suggestions) comes from AI
- **Selective integration** (which ideas to adopt, how to express them) returns to human control
- **Authorship emerges from the integration process** rather than isolated generation

This aligns with distributed cognition theory (Hutchins, 1995), which emphasizes that **cognitive processes extend across individuals and artifacts**. Creativity in human-AI collaboration becomes **distributed across the system**—neither solely human nor solely computational, but emerging from their interaction. The key factor determining whether this feels collaborative or alienating: **who controls integration decisions**.

Type B interactions concentrate control in AI (presenting preset options), creating alienation despite human selection. Type C interactions distribute control across human and AI (AI provokes, human integrates), creating collaboration despite AI contribution. The difference lies not in *how much* AI participates but in *how* participation is structured. **Authorship perception follows agency distribution, not contribution quantity**.

### 3.2 The Scaffolding Paradox: When More Support Produces Worse Outcomes

Our "helpful but alienating" paradox (Finding 2.1) represents a specific instance of a broader pedagogical challenge: **optimal support for task completion may differ from optimal support for learning**. Type B interactions maximize immediate task success (enabling poem completion, reducing cognitive load) while potentially minimizing developmental outcomes (creative agency, generative capacity, authentic voice).

This echoes long-standing debates in scaffolding research. Wood et al. (1976) warned that scaffolds providing too much direction can create **learned helplessness** rather than competence. Puntambekar and Hübscher (2005) emphasized that effective scaffolds must **preserve cognitive challenge** while reducing extraneous load. Our findings operationalize this principle in AI contexts: **structured parameters over-scaffold by eliminating generative challenge**, leaving learners with only evaluative demands (choosing among options).

**The Temporal Dimension: Scaffolding Fading Through Parameter Progression**

One resolution to this paradox emerges from our participants' panel discussion insights. The Room D participant who proposed sequencing—"Type A and B are helpful in the beginning... whereas Type C would be helpful in the end"—intuitively grasped **scaffolding fading principles**. This suggests pedagogical applications:

**Phase 1 (Novice/Early Tasks): Structured Parameters**
- Type A + Type B dominance provides accessible entry
- Learners develop basic competence, familiarize with genre conventions
- Cognitive load minimized while confidence builds

**Phase 2 (Developing/Middle Tasks): Moderate Parameters**
- Balanced interaction type distribution
- Learners begin exercising selective uptake, experiencing surprise
- Scaffolding gradually fades as agency increases

**Phase 3 (Advanced/Late Tasks): Exploratory Parameters**
- Type C elevation supports creative risk-taking
- Learners exercise full generative capacity with provocative support
- Minimal scaffolding preserves autonomy while enabling serendipity

This **progressive parameter adjustment** operationalizes scaffolding fading through systematic configuration changes rather than requiring constant human monitoring. It also addresses the paradox by **matching scaffolding intensity to developmental readiness**: structured parameters help when learners most need support, exploratory parameters challenge when learners can handle autonomy.

### 3.3 From Static Tools to Adaptive Pedagogical Partners

The most profound implication of our findings: **AI can function as adaptive pedagogical partner through parameter-aware design**. Current commercial tools (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini) treat parameters as fixed technical settings, hidden from users and rarely adjusted. This design philosophy assumes one-size-fits-all interaction patterns suffice across diverse learning contexts—a pedagogically naïve assumption our findings directly challenge.

**Toward Parameter-Literate Educational AI Design:**

Future AI writing assistants should:

1. **Expose parameters to educators** with pedagogical guidance (e.g., "Higher creativity → more surprises, lower authorship risk")
2. **Enable dynamic parameter adjustment** across learning phases, tasks, or student readiness levels
3. **Provide interaction type analytics** showing Type A/B/C distributions to support metacognitive reflection
4. **Support parameter experimentation** allowing learners to compare different configurations and develop tool literacy

This vision transforms AI from **passive response generator** to **configurable pedagogical scaffold** whose affordances can be deliberately shaped to serve learning goals. It requires **parameter literacy** among educators—understanding how temperature, top-p, and other settings influence interaction patterns and learning outcomes. But this literacy is achievable, and our study demonstrates its pedagogical value.

**The Broader Shift: From Tool Users to Tool Designers**

Ultimately, parameter-aware AI design invites educators and learners to become **co-designers of their technological environments** rather than passive consumers of fixed tools. This aligns with participatory design principles in educational technology (Penuel et al., 2007) and recognizes that **pedagogical expertise should shape technological affordances**, not vice versa. When educators understand that temperature settings determine whether AI constrains or provokes creativity, they gain agency over a powerful design variable previously hidden in engineering specifications.

This shift matters for Crosthwaite's challenge. AI threatens DDL only when deployed uncritically with hidden, potentially inappropriate configurations. AI extends DDL when educators exercise informed control over its pedagogical parameters, calibrating scaffolding intensity to serve learning goals rather than accepting default settings optimized for consumer convenience.

## 4. Limitations and Future Directions

### 4.1 Sample Size and Preliminary Status

Our findings represent **Session 1 analysis with N=10 participants** (target N=20). While current results show large effect sizes (η² = .58 for parameter effects, Cramér's V = .38 for interaction type distributions) and statistical significance (*p* < .001-.05), they remain **preliminary pending full dataset completion**. Session 2 data collection will enable:

- Validation of effect size stability across larger sample
- More precise confidence intervals for correlation estimates
- Exploration of individual difference variables (proficiency, creative writing experience, AI familiarity)
- Analysis of potential order effects or session-specific variations

Readers should interpret current findings as **robust preliminary evidence** requiring confirmation rather than definitive conclusions. The consistency between quantitative patterns and qualitative discourse evidence increases confidence, but replication remains essential.

### 4.2 Single Task Context: Poetry Writing Specificity

Our study examined **L2 poetry writing exclusively**—a creative task with particular characteristics (brevity, metaphorical language, emotional expression, formal conventions). Parameter effects might differ across:

- **Academic writing** where conventions constrain creativity more than poetry
- **Professional communication** where accuracy and clarity outweigh originality
- **Narrative fiction** where longer texts require sustained coherence
- **Multimodal composition** where visual-textual integration adds complexity

Future research should investigate **task-parameter interactions**: Which tasks benefit from exploratory parameters? When does structured scaffolding optimize rather than constrain learning? How do genre conventions mediate AI's appropriate role?

### 4.3 Single Parameter Configuration Per Room

Our 2×2 design compared **structured (0.3/0.4) versus exploratory (0.8/0.9) parameters** as fixed conditions. This establishes proof-of-concept but doesn't examine:

- **Progressive parameter adjustment** within single sessions (scaffolding fading)
- **Participant-controlled parameters** where learners adjust settings based on perceived needs
- **Intermediate parameter ranges** (e.g., temperature 0.5-0.6) as moderate scaffolding
- **Parameter combinations** testing temperature and top-p independently

Future studies enabling **dynamic parameter manipulation** during writing sessions would reveal whether scaffolding fading through progressive adjustment produces superior outcomes compared to fixed configurations.

### 4.4 Language and Cultural Context

Our study involved **English L2 learners at a Hong Kong university**—a specific linguistic and cultural context. Parameter effects might vary across:

- **Different L2s** with varying corpus training data quality for the LLM
- **Different L1-L2 combinations** affecting metaphor transfer and creative expression patterns
- **Different cultural contexts** where poetry conventions, educational norms, or AI attitudes differ
- **Different proficiency levels** potentially moderating readiness for exploratory parameters

Cross-linguistic and cross-cultural studies would establish whether our findings generalize or require context-specific calibration.

### 4.5 Long-Term Developmental Outcomes

Our study measured **immediate authorship perception and creative satisfaction** during 45-minute sessions. Critical questions remain about long-term effects:

- Does sustained Type B exposure create **dependency on AI exemplars**?
- Does Type C engagement build **transferable creative competence**?
- Do participants develop **parameter literacy** enabling adaptive tool use?
- How do repeated exposures across multiple tasks affect learning trajectories?

Longitudinal studies tracking learners across multiple AI-assisted writing experiences would reveal whether parameter effects on immediate perception translate into sustained developmental outcomes or fade over time.

### 4.6 Comparison with Traditional Instruction

Our study compared **parameter configurations within AI-assisted writing** but did not include **non-AI control conditions**. This design isolates parameter effects but cannot establish whether AI-assisted writing (regardless of parameters) outperforms traditional poetry pedagogy in producing creative outcomes, language development, or affective engagement.

Future studies should include:

- **Traditional teacher-scaffolded poetry writing** without AI
- **Peer collaboration conditions** for comparison with human-AI collaboration
- **Static exemplar provision** (corpus-based without generative AI) to isolate AI's unique contribution

Such comparisons would contextualize AI's role within broader pedagogical options rather than treating it as the sole alternative.

## 5. Pedagogical Implications: Designing for Dynamic DDL Scaffolding

### 5.1 For Educators: Parameter Literacy as Essential Competence

Our findings establish **parameter literacy** (understanding how temperature, top-p, and similar settings shape AI behavior) as **essential professional competence** for educators integrating generative AI into L2 writing instruction. This literacy includes:

**Conceptual Knowledge:**
- Temperature controls output randomness (low → deterministic/corpus-typical, high → variable/corpus-divergent)
- Top-p (nucleus sampling) controls probability mass considered (low → conservative, high → exploratory)
- These settings jointly determine whether AI constrains toward conventions or enables creative divergence

**Pedagogical Judgment:**
- When to use structured parameters (novice learners, form-focused tasks, confidence-building phases)
- When to use exploratory parameters (advanced learners, creative challenges, agency-building goals)
- How to explain parameter effects to learners if metacognitive instruction is desired

**Practical Implementation:**
- Identifying which AI tools expose parameters for educator control
- Developing assignment designs that specify appropriate parameter ranges
- Creating reflection prompts helping learners notice interaction pattern differences

This literacy requirement has implications for **teacher professional development**: workshops, courses, and resources supporting educators' understanding of AI's configurable nature become essential infrastructure for responsible technology integration.

### 5.2 For Instructional Designers: Exposing Parameters with Pedagogical Guidance

Current commercial AI writing assistants **hide parameters behind default settings** optimized for general-purpose use rather than pedagogical appropriateness. Our findings suggest educational AI tools should:

**Make Parameters Visible and Adjustable:**
- Expose temperature and top-p sliders with real-time preview of effect
- Provide preset configurations labeled by pedagogical function ("Form Practice," "Creative Exploration," "Balanced Support")
- Enable saving parameter profiles for different assignment types

**Provide Pedagogical Guidance:**
- Explain parameter effects in educator-friendly language (avoid technical jargon)
- Suggest appropriate ranges for common task types and learning goals
- Include case examples showing how parameter choices shape student experiences

**Support Reflective Practice:**
- Display interaction type analytics (% Type A/B/C) to make scaffolding intensity visible
- Enable A/B comparison where students try different parameter settings
- Provide discussion prompts exploring authorship perception across configurations

These design features would **democratize parameter literacy**, making powerful pedagogical levers accessible to educators without requiring technical expertise.

### 5.3 For Curriculum Developers: Sequencing Parameter Intensity

Our participants' intuitive grasp of scaffolding fading principles suggests **curricular sequencing** as a promising application:

**Progressive Parameter Curriculum:**

*Unit 1 (Weeks 1-3): Building Foundations*
- Structured parameters (0.3/0.4)
- Focus: Mastering poetic forms, conventions, basic devices
- Outcome: Confidence, technical competence, reduced anxiety

*Unit 2 (Weeks 4-6): Developing Voice*
- Moderate parameters (0.5/0.6)
- Focus: Balancing convention and originality, selective uptake practice
- Outcome: Beginning authorial agency, discriminative judgment

*Unit 3 (Weeks 7-9): Creative Independence*
- Exploratory parameters (0.8/0.9)
- Focus: Risk-taking, serendipitous discovery, authentic expression
- Outcome: Creative agency, parameter awareness, transfer readiness

This **systematic fading through parameter adjustment** operationalizes scaffolding principles without requiring constant instructor monitoring. It also builds **learner parameter literacy gradually**, enabling eventual self-scaffolding.

### 5.4 For Learners: Developing Metacognitive Tool Awareness

While Finding 3.1 showed awareness didn't moderate immediate authorship perception, Finding 3.3 revealed aware participants demonstrated **strategic parameter understanding**. This suggests value in **metacognitive instruction** helping learners recognize:

**How AI Behavior Varies:**
- "Sometimes AI gives you exact options to choose from"
- "Sometimes AI surprises you with ideas you didn't expect"
- "The tool works differently depending on settings"

**How to Match Tools to Goals:**
- "If you want help with grammar, use lower creativity settings"
- "If you want fresh ideas, try higher creativity settings"
- "You can adjust settings as you write based on what you need"

**How to Maintain Agency:**
- "You always decide whether to use AI suggestions"
- "Your ideas are still yours even if AI helps you express them"
- "Being an author means making choices, not generating everything alone"

This **metacognitive scaffolding** supports learners' development as **adaptive technology users** capable of configuring tools to serve their intentions rather than passively accepting default behaviors.

## 6. Conclusion: Parameter Configuration as the Hinge of DDL's Future

We opened this discussion with Crosthwaite's (2023) challenge: Does generative AI represent the death of Data-Driven Learning? Our findings provide a definitive answer: **AI represents neither DDL's death nor its simple continuation, but a pedagogical hinge where parameter configuration determines which future unfolds**.

When deployed with **structured parameters** (low temperature, low top-p), AI functions as **DDL antithesis**—generating corpus-typical exemplars that position learners as selectors rather than discoverers, undermining the inductive engagement and creative agency central to DDL pedagogy. This configuration validates Crosthwaite's concern: AI does the work for learners, creating "helpful but alienating" experiences where task completion occurs at the expense of authorship perception.

When deployed with **exploratory parameters** (high temperature, high top-p), AI functions as **DDL evolution**—generating corpus-extending provocations that position learners as co-constructors discovering unexpected possibilities. This configuration transcends traditional DDL limitations by enabling pattern-based induction on generative rather than static corpora, preserving creative agency while providing transformative support. Learners exercise selective uptake, critical judgment, and linguistic instantiation—capacities central to both DDL and creative development.

**The mechanism linking these configurations to pedagogical outcomes**—the Parameter→Interaction→Perception pathway validated through our findings—establishes **parameter literacy as essential competence** for educators navigating AI integration. Temperature and top-p settings are not technical minutiae but pedagogical design variables determining whether AI constrains or enables, whether it scaffolds appropriately or over-determines outcomes.

This insight transforms how we conceptualize responsible AI integration in language education. The challenge is not whether to use AI (its presence is inevitable) but **how to configure AI** to serve learning goals. This requires:

- **Educator parameter literacy**: Understanding how settings shape interaction patterns
- **Exposed, adjustable parameters**: Tools designed for pedagogical control rather than hidden defaults
- **Adaptive parameter adjustment**: Scaffolding fading through progressive configuration changes
- **Learner metacognitive development**: Building awareness of tool variability and strategic adaptation

DDL's future in the age of generative AI depends not on AI's capabilities but on **our capacity to exercise pedagogical judgment over its configuration**. The death Crosthwaite feared would come not from AI's existence but from our failure to understand and control how it shapes learning transactions. The evolution we demonstrate becomes possible when educators recognize **parameters as pedagogical levers** and design learning environments accordingly.

In this light, our study's most consequential contribution may be demonstrating that **generative AI is not a pedagogical intervention but a configurable platform** whose affordances must be deliberately shaped through informed design choices. The temperature dial and top-p slider—seemingly technical specifications—emerge as **pivotal instruments determining whether technology serves or subverts learning**. Understanding this, educators gain agency over AI's role in DDL's next chapter.

The death of DDL remains possible if we deploy AI blindly, accepting default configurations optimized for commercial convenience rather than pedagogical appropriateness. But DDL's evolution becomes achievable—even inevitable—when we recognize that **the future of corpus-based pedagogy hinges not on AI's power but on our wisdom in wielding it**. This wisdom begins with a simple recognition: every parameter setting embeds a pedagogical philosophy, and our responsibility lies in ensuring that philosophy aligns with learning values we hold dear.

Parameter configuration is not merely technical implementation—it is **pedagogical decision-making disguised as engineering specification**. Making it visible, adjustable, and pedagogically grounded transforms AI from potential threat into powerful partner in DDL's ongoing evolution. This transformation requires literacy, intentionality, and courage to question default assumptions about how AI should function in educational contexts. Our findings suggest the effort is worthwhile: the difference between alienation and authorship, between constraint and creativity, between DDL's death and its evolution—may hinge on something as seemingly mundane as adjusting a temperature setting from 0.3 to 0.8.

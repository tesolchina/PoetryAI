# Discussion: Parameter Configuration as Pedagogical Watershed in AI-Assisted L2 Creative Writing

**Word Count:** ~3,000 words

## Opening: Resolving Crosthwaite's Challenge Through Parameter Literacy

When Crosthwaite (2023) posed the provocative question of whether generative AI represents "the death of Data-Driven Learning," the implicit assumption embedded in his challenge was that AI's capabilities would determine the answer—that the technology itself carries inherent pedagogical consequences independent of human design. Our three-phase study, spanning thirty L2 learners across experimental manipulation, voluntary workshop participation, and fully autonomous exploration, reveals a fundamentally different truth: **generative AI represents neither DDL's death nor its simple continuation, but a pedagogical watershed where parameter configuration determines which path unfolds**.

The findings we have presented—the seven-fold increase in Type C "surprise harvest" interactions when shifting from structured to exploratory parameters (5% to 35%, χ²=24.3, p<.001, Cramér's V=.38), the persistent "helpful yet alienating" paradox of Type B exemplar-giving, the emergence of procedural ownership through Type C's iterative reciprocity, and autonomy's role as meta-variable transforming identical interactions from frustration to satisfaction—converge on a singular insight: **parameter settings function not as technical specifications but as pedagogical design levers** that fundamentally reshape the learning transaction between human creativity and computational capability.

This discussion interprets our cross-phase findings through four interconnected lenses: (1) how the Parameter→Interaction→Perception mechanism operationalizes dynamic DDL scaffolding, (2) why the Type B paradox reveals fundamental tensions between surface and deep learning in AI-mediated creative pedagogy, (3) how Type C interactions enable DDL-compatible collaboration through corpus-extending provocation, and (4) what these mechanisms suggest for designing AI tools that preserve learner agency while providing transformative support. Through this analysis, we answer Crosthwaite's challenge not with a binary verdict but with a conditional framework: DDL's future depends not solely on AI's capabilities but on educators' capacity to recognize and exercise pedagogical judgment over parameter configuration.

## The Parameter→Interaction→Perception Pathway: Operationalizing Dynamic DDL Scaffolding

### From Technical Settings to Pedagogical Affordances

The seven-fold increase in Type C interactions when moving from structured (T=0.3/Top-p=0.4) to exploratory parameters (T=0.8/Top-p=0.9) represents more than a statistically significant finding—it demonstrates **parameter configuration as a pedagogical lever with immediate, measurable effects on learning affordances**. This is not subtle influence requiring sustained exposure to detect; it is fundamental transformation occurring within individual creative sessions. When parameters shift from low to high, AI's phenomenological character metamorphoses entirely: from patient tutor enforcing corpus-typical patterns to imaginative collaborator extending beyond predictable conventions, from curator of pre-existing options to generator of unexpected provocations.

Consider what participants encountered viscerally across our three phases. In structured rooms where low parameters constrained AI to high-probability token selections, learners met an AI that behaved as meticulous editor—catching deviations from conventional patterns, offering polished alternatives drawn from training data frequencies, providing linguistic safety nets that prevented errors but also constrained creative risk-taking. The experience felt orderly, predictable, educational: as p 05 described it, "It took our words and put it in poem form"—AI as reformatter and refiner, transforming raw expression into technically competent verse. The interaction ecology became dominated by Type A constraint repair (60%) and Type B exemplar selection (35%), creating what participants characterized as "grammar checker" (p 06) and "mechanical" (multiple participants) assistance.

In exploratory rooms where high parameters enabled broader token sampling, learners encountered something qualitatively different: an AI that posed philosophical questions about their creative intentions, suggested unexpected craft techniques like "found poetry" incorporating legal citations, ventured creative risks through surprising metaphors, and responded to emotional vulnerability with nuanced recognition rather than formulaic consolation. P 12 articulated the phenomenological shift precisely: AI "gives me random non-related stuff so I could think more about it"—not predetermined options to select but provocations to contemplate, evaluate, and selectively integrate. The relationship transformed from functional tool use to what p 16 called "guiding it through each step"—active directional control through iterative negotiation.

This experiential difference operationalizes what traditional Data-Driven Learning could never achieve: **adaptive pattern engagement intensity calibrated through system configuration**. Johns' (1991) DDL vision positioned learners as active "research workers" discovering patterns through concordance interrogation, but engagement intensity remained essentially uniform—learners encountered whatever patterns their queries surfaced from static corpora. AI-mediated DDL enables **system-initiated engagement intensity modulation** where parameter settings determine whether learners encounter predominantly corpus-typical patterns (structured parameters prioritizing high-frequency, conventional outputs) or corpus-extending provocations (exploratory parameters sampling from broader, more diverse token distributions including lower-frequency creative possibilities).

### Why This Represents DDL Evolution, Not Replacement

The crucial theoretical recognition is that this transformation preserves DDL's foundational insight while expanding its operational possibilities. Boulton (2012) emphasized that DDL's pedagogical power stems from "hands-on" engagement where learners actively interrogate corpora, formulate hypotheses, test patterns, and internalize discoveries through sustained interaction. The pedagogical mechanism isn't merely exposure to patterns but **active construction of understanding through pattern exploration**—an insight grounded in constructivist learning theory (Piaget, 1952) and sociocultural approaches to second language acquisition emphasizing learner agency (Lantolf & Thorne, 2006).

Exploratory parameters preserve this active construction while shifting its locus. Instead of learners searching static corpora for patterns that already exist in training data, they engage in **selective uptake and critical evaluation of system-initiated provocations** that extend beyond corpus frequencies through creative recombination. The "hands-on" engagement doesn't disappear—it transforms from pattern discovery through corpus querying to pattern synthesis through provocation evaluation. Learners exercise agency not by deciding what to search for but by deciding what to accept, reject, modify, and integrate from AI's generative suggestions.

This shift addresses a fundamental limitation of traditional corpus DDL that Crosthwaite (2023) himself acknowledged: corpus data represents **patterns of what has been written**, not necessarily **what could be written**—a constraint particularly salient in creative writing where novelty and originality are pedagogical goals. When we confine learners to corpus-typical patterns, we risk cultivating reproductive competence (ability to recognize and replicate conventional forms) at the expense of generative capacity (ability to create novel expressions extending beyond training data). Exploratory parameters enable AI to function as **pattern-extender** rather than **pattern-enforcer**, generating possibilities through creative divergence that learners evaluate critically rather than accept passively.

The evidence for this preserved agency appears vividly in Type C interaction sequences from our chat logs. P 12's creative journey—from initial vision ("cursed princess in dragon form") through critical evaluation ("I don't want receptive use of the word whisper") to narrative clarification ("The princess seeks the witch's help, not vice versa")—demonstrates learners exercising directional control through rejection-refinement cycles. Each exchange represented active guidance rather than passive selection, with participants steering AI toward their creative vision through evaluative feedback. This iterative reciprocity created what p 12 experienced as 90% self-authorship despite 15-20% AI word contribution—the highest ownership perception across all thirty participants precisely because procedural control remained consistently in human hands.

## The Type B Paradox: When Helpfulness Undermines Learning Through Surface Engagement

### The Paradox That Haunted Three Phases

Perhaps the most theoretically consequential finding that emerged from our cross-phase investigation was the persistent contradiction surrounding Type B exemplar-giving interactions: participants consistently rated Type B "most helpful" (75% in Phase 1, declining to 50% in Phase 2 and 33% in Phase 3 as autonomy increased), acknowledging its immediate utility for task completion. Yet those who experienced predominantly Type B encounters—learners in structured parameter conditions where AI presented curated option sets requiring selection—reported dramatically diminished authorship perception (10-35% range) and lower creative satisfaction (M=3.2-4.0/5 compared to 4.75/5 in voluntary contexts). High utility coexisted with low ownership; immediate helpfulness predicted long-term dissatisfaction.

The paradox proved remarkably stable across methodological variations. It manifested in Phase 1's controlled experimental conditions where parameters were imposed, persisted through Phase 2's naturalistic workshop where learners chose parameter conditions voluntarily, and remained visible in Phase 3's fully autonomous exploration. Even when participants **selected** Type B interactions strategically for specific purposes—grammar confidence building, vocabulary expansion, learning formal structures—the negative correlation with authorship (r=-.58) held steady. This cross-context stability suggests we are observing not methodological artifact or demand characteristic but a **fundamental tension inherent in exemplar-driven AI scaffolding**.

The paradox manifested most poignantly in participants' own words. P 05 captured the alienation viscerally: "It feels like just AI... Ours took our words and put it in poem form... I couldn't feel any sort of creativity"—the plaintive sense of being reduced from creator to content provider, watching AI transform raw feeling into polished verse without genuine collaboration. P 06 mourned the loss of emotional authenticity: "It felt more true and emotional before the AI. After it became more formulaic and didn't really capture it"—recognizing that technical competence achieved through exemplar selection came at the expense of personal voice. These weren't complaints about AI's technical limitations; they represented deeper phenomenological experiences of creative displacement.

Yet these same participants acknowledged Type B's pragmatic value. P 14, who maintained merely 10% authorship claim, nevertheless reported maximum satisfaction (5/5) and rated Type B most helpful: "I found the AI helpful for exploring alternatives... it helped me build confidence in choosing words." P 15 articulated what we might call legitimate peripheral participation in linguistic competence: "In English, I find it more natural to use AI for writing assistance since English isn't my primary language"—framing AI support not as creative displacement but as scaffolding that fills L2 gaps without threatening conceptual ownership.

### Surface Learning vs. Deep Learning: The Theoretical Framework

To understand why helpfulness and alienation coexist so persistently, we must examine the cognitive processes Type B interactions elicit through the lens of **surface versus deep learning** (Marton & Säljö, 1976; Biggs, 1987). This distinction, foundational to educational psychology, illuminates precisely what Type B optimizes and what it undermines.

**Surface learning** occurs when learners focus on reproducing information or applying procedures mechanically—memorizing exemplars without understanding their generative principles, selecting from preset options without interrogating how patterns work or why certain choices create particular effects, mimicking models without engaging the underlying relationships between form and meaning. Surface learning can produce successful task performance in the immediate moment—learners complete assignments, generate technically competent outputs, satisfy formal requirements—but it develops limited transferable capacity because understanding remains instrumental and procedural rather than conceptual and generative.

**Deep learning** occurs when learners interrogate patterns to understand their structural logic, explore relationships between concepts to grasp how systems function, actively construct meaning through hypothesis testing and revision, and integrate new knowledge into existing conceptual frameworks transforming both. Deep learning requires cognitive struggle—sustained engagement with complexity, tolerance for ambiguity during exploration, willingness to fail and revise—but it develops robust, transferable competence because understanding becomes principled rather than merely procedural.

Type B interactions, by their fundamental architecture, promote **surface-learning orientations**. When AI presents predetermined exemplars that learners can select and use without interrogating how the patterns work or why certain choices matter, the cognitive process becomes **reproductive selection** rather than **generative construction**. Learners engage in pattern-application (choosing appropriate exemplars from available options) rather than pattern-discovery (understanding why patterns work and how to create novel instances). The mental operation resembles selecting items from a menu rather than cooking from recipes—useful for immediate consumption but developing limited culinary capacity.

Consider the specific cognitive moves Type B elicits, as evidenced in our chat transcripts from structured parameter rooms:

1. **AI presents option set**: "Here are a few options for your next line: (a) Rabbits scurry with visions in tow; (b) Machines hum softly, stitching joy and woe; (c) Every dream takes shape, in colors bright and bold"

2. **Learner evaluates superficially**: Which sounds best? Which fits the rhyme scheme? Which feels closest to my vague intention?

3. **Learner selects**: "I'll use option (b)"

4. **Pattern incorporated without deep processing**: The chosen line gets integrated into the poem without sustained interrogation of why "stitching" creates particular effects, how "joy and woe" functions as binary construction, or what alternatives might exist beyond the three options presented.

This sequence optimizes for task efficiency—learners rapidly assemble competent-looking poems—but bypasses the transformative struggle that builds creative and linguistic capacity. The learner never grapples with the generative question: *How do I create a line that captures my specific meaning with distinctive voice?* Instead, they answer the selective question: *Which pre-made line fits adequately?* The cognitive demand drops from generation to evaluation, from synthesis to selection.

### Why This Contradicts DDL Principles and Creative Writing Pedagogy

The contradiction with DDL principles becomes clear when we recall Boulton's (2012) emphasis that DDL's pedagogical power stems from learners actively interrogating corpora, formulating hypotheses, testing patterns, and internalizing discoveries through sustained interaction. This active construction differentiates DDL from passive rule presentation and underlies its demonstrated effectiveness for language learning. Type B interactions reverse this dynamic: instead of learners constructing understanding from patterns, AI constructs options and learners choose. The "hands-on" engagement central to DDL pedagogy becomes "hands-off" selection from pre-packaged alternatives.

The contradiction with creative writing pedagogy proves equally fundamental. Hanauer (2010) argues that L2 poetry writing's developmental value lies in cultivating **authentic voice** and **creative agency**—capacities requiring learners to struggle with expression, experiment with possibilities beyond their current comfort zone, and interrogate linguistic choices deeply enough to discover their linguistic-creative identity. These developmental outcomes emerge not from reproducing exemplary language but from **the struggle to make language do things it hasn't done before**, from **failing and revising** until expression approximates intention. As Kerbs, McQueston, and Lawrance (2024) emphasize in their work on scaffolding writing through poetry, effective support provides structure without removing productive struggle—offering constraints that focus creative energy rather than templates that eliminate creative decision-making.

Type B's exemplar-selection model **short-circuits this developmental process**. Learners may produce technically competent poems—poems that scan properly, rhyme appropriately, use vivid imagery—but they do so through **reproductive curation** (selecting and assembling AI-generated components) rather than **generative creation** (constructing language through deep engagement with meaning-making). They internalize patterns at the surface level, recognizing good lines when presented but not developing deep understanding of **how** language creates effects, **why** certain choices work in specific contexts, or **what alternatives** exist beyond the options AI happened to generate.

The authorship alienation participants expressed—"I couldn't feel any sort of creativity" (p 05), "It doesn't feel like mine" (p 06), "I just need to choose use what sentence wrote by AI. I only own the meaning on this poem" (p 14)—reflects not mere emotional dissatisfaction but accurate metacognitive assessment. These learners recognized that while they completed the poetry-writing task successfully, **they had not engaged in the generative cognitive work that constitutes creative authorship**. The mental operations they performed resembled editing and selection more than composition and creation. Task achievement occurred, but transformative learning did not.

### Why the Paradox Transformed Across Autonomy Contexts

Yet here emerges the theoretically crucial nuance that our three-phase design revealed: **while Type B's surface-learning orientation remained constant across contexts, its emotional signature and subjective acceptability transformed dramatically** depending on whether learners encountered it through imposed assignment or voluntary choice. The 0.75-point satisfaction increase from Phase 1 (imposed parameters, M=3.5/5) to Phases 2-3 (voluntary selection, M=4.75/5) captures this transformation quantitatively, but participants' discourse reveals the mechanism qualitatively.

When Type B arrived as imposed scaffolding in Phase 1's experimental rooms, learners experienced it as **having creative agency removed**—someone else (the researchers, the system designers) decided they needed this much support, this level of constraint, this particular style of assistance. Type B became a symbol of diminished creative control even when objectively helpful. Frustration emerged not from Type B's inherent properties but from the **lack of choice** about encountering it. The experience felt infantilizing: "It only ever outputted exactly what I asked it to" (p 06)—AI as over-controlling parent who won't let the child make their own mistakes.

When Type B arrived as chosen tool in Phases 2-3's voluntary contexts, learners experienced it as **exercising agency through strategic tool selection**. P 14's experience demonstrates this transformation perfectly: maintaining merely 10% authorship yet reporting maximum satisfaction because they **chose** Type B deliberately for vocabulary confidence building, maintaining metacognitive clarity about what they were ceding to AI (linguistic realization) versus retaining (conceptual direction). The identical AI behavior—presenting curated options requiring selection—now felt like pragmatic resource use rather than creative displacement. Participants accepted lower authorship as reasonable trade-off for targeted skill development in domains where they recognized their L2 limitations.

This reveals that **Type B paradox stems not from the interaction type itself but from how autonomy moderates the experience of scaffolding constraint**. Surface learning remains surface learning whether imposed or chosen—the cognitive processes Type B elicits don't fundamentally change. But the **affective experience and subjective acceptability** of surface learning transforms entirely when learners exercise choice. They can pragmatically accept that sometimes efficiency and task completion matter more than deep creative struggle, but only when **they decide** when those trade-offs are appropriate rather than having the decision imposed externally.

## Type C Interactions: DDL-Compatible Collaboration Through Corpus-Extending Provocation

### Procedural Ownership Through Iterative Reciprocity

If Type B represents the dark side of AI scaffolding—immediate utility purchased at authorship's expense—Type C interactions illuminate its transformative potential. Across all three phases, participants who experienced high frequencies of Type C "surprise harvest" interactions demonstrated a pattern that initially appeared paradoxical but ultimately revealed a different ownership architecture: they claimed substantial authorship (60-96% range) despite extensive AI involvement in final texts, reported elevated creative satisfaction (M=4.5-5.0/5), and described AI partners using relational rather than instrumental language ("very good friend" rather than "grammar checker").

The mechanism underlying this seemingly contradictory pattern proved to be **iterative reciprocity**—ownership emerging not from proportional word contribution but from **directional control exercised through rejection-refinement cycles**. P 12's creative journey in Phase 2 demonstrates this architecture with exceptional clarity. Despite AI contributing an estimated 15-20% of final words, this participant claimed 90% self-authorship—the highest across all thirty participants. The chat logs reveal why: five distinct iterative cycles where the participant maintained directional control throughout:

1. **Initial creative vision** (entirely human-generated): "I want to write about a cursed princess in dragon form"
2. **AI's first attempt**: Opening lines about transformation and curse
3. **Critical evaluation** (rejecting while specifying): "I don't want receptive use of the word whisper"—accepting structural approach while rejecting specific linguistic choice
4. **AI refinement** (incorporating feedback): Adjusted to "hidden hope" while maintaining poetic register
5. **Narrative clarification** (correcting misunderstanding): "The princess seeks the witch's help, not vice versa"—redirecting plot logic and character agency

Each cycle represented **active guidance** rather than passive selection. P 12 wasn't choosing from pre-made options but steering AI toward their creative vision through evaluative feedback, rejecting suggestions that missed the mark, refining those that came close, synthesizing unexpected provocations into coherent expression. The collaboration felt genuinely mutual: participant controlled emotional architecture and conceptual direction; AI contributed linguistic resources and formal realization. Both exercised creativity complementarily rather than competitively.

This procedural ownership explains the inverse relationship between AI word contribution and subjective authorship claims that proved so consistent across our three phases. When AI contributes through Type C provocations requiring critical evaluation and selective integration—unexpected metaphors demanding judgment about fit, surprising connections requiring assessment of relevance, novel framings necessitating decisions about adoption—even 40-50% word contribution feels like collaboration rather than displacement because **the learner exercises directional control at every decision point**. Each rejection-refinement cycle becomes what we might call a **micro-assertion of creative authority**, accumulating into profound ownership despite substantial AI involvement.

Contrast this with Type B's passive selection model where even 15-20% AI word contribution feels like creative abdication because learners never exercise generative control—only evaluative selection from predetermined options. The cognitive architecture differs fundamentally: Type C demands **synthesis** (how do I integrate this unexpected suggestion into my evolving vision?), while Type B requires only **selection** (which pre-made option fits adequately?). Synthesis develops creative capacity; selection merely exercises choice among externally generated possibilities.

### Multi-Dimensional Authorship: Beyond Scalar Metrics

Our Phase 2-3 analysis revealed that authorship doesn't operate as single scalar quantity ("60% mine, 40% AI") but across **multiple independent dimensions** with distinct patterns of human control and AI support. This dimensional structure explains how substantial AI involvement can coexist with high authorship claims without paradox:

**Concept/Theme** (100% human control across all participants): The fundamental "aboutness" of the poem—what it explores, what questions it poses, what emotions it examines—remained universally human-controlled even when AI contributed substantially to linguistic realization. No participant reported AI generating their core creative vision; they generated concepts and invited AI to help express them.

**Emotional Direction** (~80% human control): The emotional arc, affective tone, and vulnerability level stayed predominantly human-directed. AI might offer refinement suggestions ("Do you want this to condemn or understand?"), but participants determined whether their poems would rage or mourn, indict or empathize, celebrate or lament.

**Form/Structure** (50-50 split with diverse individual preferences): Some participants exercised tight control over formal elements (line breaks, stanza structure, rhyme schemes), while others welcomed AI's structural suggestions. This dimension showed the most individual variation, reflecting different creative priorities.

**Language/Words** (~70% AI in L2 contexts): In the domain where L2 learners felt least confident, AI contributed most substantially—surfacing vocabulary beyond participants' active lexicons, suggesting collocations they wouldn't have discovered independently, generating phrases with target-language fluency they couldn't yet produce autonomously.

**Final Decision Authority** (100% human control): Universally, participants retained ultimate approval over what entered their poems. No suggestion became final text without human acceptance, and participants freely rejected, modified, or replaced AI contributions.

This dimensional analysis reveals why single percentage metrics mislead. When p 12 claimed 90% self-authorship despite 20% AI word contribution, they weren't miscalculating but accurately reflecting that **they controlled the dimensions they valued most for creative identity**—concept, emotional direction, and final decision authority—while pragmatically accepting AI support for linguistic realization where their L2 status created genuine capability gaps. Authentic creative voice emerged from **directional control over conceptual and emotional dimensions**, not from generating every word independently.

### How Type C Preserves and Extends DDL Principles

Type C interactions resolve the apparent contradiction between AI assistance and DDL principles by transforming AI from pattern-retriever to **pattern-extender**. Traditional corpus DDL operates within what we might call **corpus-bounded possibility space**—discovering patterns that exist in training data, frequencies that reflect what has been written. This proves invaluable for developing corpus-typical competence (knowing what educated native writers conventionally do) but constrains creative exploration to reproducing existing patterns.

Type C, enabled through exploratory parameters that sample from broader token distributions, **extends beyond corpus frequencies into generative possibility space**. The "random non-related stuff" that p 12 valued represents AI's capacity to suggest connections, techniques, and framings emerging through novel pattern recombination rather than corpus retrieval. This generates provocations that learners **could not** have discovered through traditional concordance searches because they don't exist as high-frequency patterns in training data—they emerge through creative divergence, through AI's capacity to combine elements in unexpected ways.

Critically, this extension preserves DDL's core insight that **patterns discovered are better learned than rules transmitted**. Type C doesn't present provocations as authoritative answers requiring acceptance; it generates possibilities demanding critical evaluation. Learners must assess relevance, judge fit, evaluate appropriateness, and selectively integrate suggestions—all cognitive operations requiring pattern understanding rather than pattern reproduction. The "hands-on" engagement central to DDL pedagogy persists, shifted from querying static corpora to evaluating dynamic provocations.

The difference between Type B and Type C becomes clear: Type B automates pattern discovery by presenting pre-selected exemplars, collapsing the exploratory space into curated options and shifting learners from discoverers to selectors. Type C **amplifies** pattern discovery by generating provocations extending beyond corpus frequencies, expanding the exploratory space beyond what concordance searches could surface and positioning learners as critical evaluators synthesizing unexpected possibilities into coherent expression. One represents DDL's death through automation; the other represents its evolution through augmentation.

## Pedagogical Implications: Parameter Literacy as Essential Professional Competence

### The Conditional Answer to Crosthwaite's Challenge

Our findings provide what we might call a **conditional resolution** to Crosthwaite's (2023) challenge about whether generative AI represents DDL's death or evolution. The answer is neither predetermined nor technologically determined—it depends entirely on how educators configure, prompt, and design pedagogical AI systems.

Under structured parameters that constrain AI to corpus-typical outputs, AI functions as **DDL's antithesis**: generating predetermined exemplars that position learners as selectors rather than discoverers, promoting surface learning through reproductive selection rather than deep learning through generative construction, enabling task completion while bypassing the transformative struggle that builds creative and linguistic capacity. This configuration validates Crosthwaite's concern—AI **would** do the work for learners, undermining the inductive engagement central to DDL pedagogy.

Under exploratory parameters that enable corpus-extending provocations, AI functions as **DDL's evolution**: generating unexpected possibilities that position learners as co-constructors exercising selective uptake and critical judgment, promoting deep learning through synthesis and integration rather than surface learning through selection, scaffolding creative expansion while demanding continuous directional control. This configuration demonstrates AI's transformative potential—AI **extends** the work available to learners, expanding the possibility space beyond what corpus consultation could provide while preserving the agency and active construction central to DDL principles.

The crucial recognition: **parameter settings are not technical specifications but pedagogical design variables** determining whether AI constrains or enables, scaffolds appropriately or over-determines outcomes, promotes surface learning or supports deep engagement. This insight transforms the question of responsible AI integration from **whether** to use AI (presence is increasingly inevitable as these tools proliferate) to **how** to configure AI serving learning goals rather than undermining them.

### Design Recommendations for Parameter-Aware Pedagogical AI

Our findings establish **parameter literacy as essential professional competence** for educators integrating AI into L2 writing instruction. This literacy encompasses three interconnected dimensions:

**Technical Understanding**: Educators need functional knowledge of how temperature and top-p settings shape AI behavior—not requiring deep technical expertise in language model architecture, but understanding that lower values produce deterministic, corpus-typical outputs while higher values enable variable, exploratory generations. This knowledge should become standard content in educational technology courses and professional development programs.

**Pedagogical Judgment**: Educators must exercise informed judgment about when structured versus exploratory parameters serve learning goals—recognizing that different creative tasks, proficiency levels, and developmental stages may require different scaffolding intensities. This judgment develops through experience experimenting with configurations and observing how learners respond.

**Tool Selection**: Educators need capacity to identify AI tools that expose parameters for educator control versus those that hide parameters as proprietary settings. This requires advocating for transparency in educational AI design and potentially rejecting tools that don't provide necessary configurability.

Educational AI platforms should embody these principles through specific design features:

**Visible, Adjustable Parameters with Pedagogical Guidance**: Rather than hiding temperature and top-p in advanced settings, platforms should make them primary design variables with clear explanations of pedagogical effects. Labels like "Structured Support" (low parameters) versus "Creative Exploration" (high parameters) help educators match configurations to learning goals without requiring technical expertise.

**Preset Configurations with Clear Functions**: Platforms might offer configurations like "Form Practice" (heavy Type A/B scaffolding for mastering conventions), "Voice Development" (balanced Type A/B/C for developing personal style), and "Creative Independence" (Type C-dominant for experienced writers ready for minimal constraint). These presets reduce decision complexity while maintaining configurability.

**Interaction Analytics for Metacognitive Reflection**: Displaying Type A/B/C distributions after sessions—showing learners what kinds of interactions occurred and prompting reflection on whether that scaffolding served their goals—builds learner parameter literacy alongside educator expertise. This transparency transforms AI from black box to transparent scaffold whose operations learners can understand and influence.

**Parameter Experimentation Support**: Allowing learners to compare different configurations on the same creative task—writing one stanza with structured parameters, another with exploratory, then reflecting on how experiences differed—develops nuanced understanding of scaffolding trade-offs and empowers informed tool selection.

### Curricular Sequencing Through Progressive Parameter Adjustment

Our findings suggest progressive parameter curriculum implementing scaffolding fade through systematic configuration adjustment:

**Weeks 1-3 (Foundation Building)**: Structured parameters (T=0.3/Top-p=0.4) provide heavy scaffolding as learners develop basic competence with poetic forms, master fundamental techniques, and build confidence with AI interaction. Type A constraint repair dominates, establishing technical foundations.

**Weeks 4-6 (Voice Development)**: Moderate parameters (T=0.5-0.6/Top-p=0.6-0.7) balance Type B exemplars with emerging Type C provocations as learners begin developing personal style while maintaining support. Scaffolding intensity decreases gradually.

**Weeks 7-9 (Creative Independence)**: Exploratory parameters (T=0.8/Top-p=0.9) enable Type C-dominant interactions as learners take creative risks with minimal constraint. AI functions as provocative collaborator rather than directive tutor.

This systematic fade operationalizes scaffolding principles (Wood et al., 1976; Van de Pol et al., 2010) without requiring constant instructor monitoring—parameter adjustment **automates** appropriate support reduction as learners develop capacity. The crucial insight: **scaffolding fade becomes adjustable through parameter manipulation** rather than requiring constant human calibration.

## Limitations and Future Directions

Our three-phase study, while providing robust cross-context evidence, carries limitations requiring acknowledgment. The sample comprised thirty L2 learners within a specific institutional context (Hong Kong Polytechnic University), limiting generalizability across diverse L2 populations, proficiency levels, cultural contexts, and educational systems. The relatively compressed timeframe (November 2025-January 2026) prevented longitudinal tracking of how parameter preferences and creative development evolve over extended engagement. Do initially Type-B-dependent learners graduate to Type C as proficiency increases? Does the novelty of exploratory parameters wear off, or does their value persist?

The poetry genre may amplify certain effects—particularly regarding authorship perception and emotional expression—that might manifest differently in academic writing, professional communication, or narrative fiction. Cross-genre studies examining how parameters function in argumentative essays (where corpus-typical patterns may be more pedagogically appropriate) or technical writing (where creativity is less valued than clarity) would illuminate whether our findings represent general principles or genre-specific dynamics.

Individual differences warrant deeper investigation. How do personality traits (openness to experience, need for cognitive closure, tolerance for ambiguity), learning styles (reflective vs. impulsive), and creative dispositions (risk-seeking vs. risk-averse) interact with parameter preferences and interaction type success? Are certain learner profiles consistently drawn to particular scaffolding modes, or does strategic adaptation override dispositional tendencies?

We need validated instruments for measuring dimensional attribution that capture authentic creative ownership beyond single percentages. Can we develop rubrics or self-report measures reliably distinguishing control over conceptual/emotional dimensions from linguistic realization, enabling more nuanced assessment of hybrid human-AI authorship? Such instruments would advance both research precision and pedagogical practice.

Finally, our study examined voluntary creative writing contexts designed to maximize emotional safety and minimize performance pressure. How do findings transfer to graded coursework where institutional evaluation introduces different motivational dynamics? Does the autonomy premium we observed (0.75-point satisfaction increase in voluntary contexts) disappear when AI assistance becomes required for assessed work?

## Conclusion: The Pedagogical Imperative

When thirty L2 learners engaged with AI-assisted poetry writing across three progressively naturalistic contexts, they revealed something fundamental about the future of language pedagogy: **generative AI represents neither the death of Data-Driven Learning nor its simple continuation, but a pedagogical watershed where parameter configuration determines which path unfolds**. With structured parameters constraining AI to corpus-typical patterns and Type B exemplar selection, AI validates Crosthwaite's concern—creating "helpful but alienating" experiences where task completion occurs at authorship's expense, where surface learning replaces deep engagement, where learners become consumers of AI-generated options rather than constructors of personal expression.

With exploratory parameters enabling corpus-extending provocations and Type C surprise harvest, AI enables **DDL evolution**—preserving and amplifying creative agency while providing transformative support through provocations that extend possibility space beyond what concordance searches could surface. Learners exercise directional control through iterative reciprocity, develop procedural ownership through rejection-refinement cycles, and maintain authentic voice through strategic selective uptake of AI suggestions. The "hands-on" engagement central to DDL pedagogy persists, transformed from querying static corpora to evaluating dynamic provocations.

The pedagogical imperative becomes clear: recognize parameter configuration as pedagogical decision-making, make it visible and adjustable, design for learner autonomy and metacognitive awareness, and prioritize low-stakes voluntary contexts enabling authentic expression. **DDL's future depends not on AI's capabilities but on educators' capacity to exercise informed pedagogical judgment over its configuration**. The death Crosthwaite feared comes not from AI's existence but from educators failing to understand and control how technical specifications shape learning experiences. The evolution we demonstrate becomes possible when educators recognize parameters as pedagogical levers and design accordingly.

Our thirty participants, through their creative journeys writing poetry with AI assistance, have illuminated a path forward—one where generative AI serves not as replacement for human creativity or threat to learner agency, but as **adaptive scaffold extending the space of creative and linguistic possibilities** while preserving the directional control that defines authentic authorship. **Parameter configuration is pedagogical decision-making disguised as technical specification.** Making it visible, adjustable, and pedagogically grounded transforms AI from potential threat into powerful partner in DDL's ongoing evolution in the generative era.

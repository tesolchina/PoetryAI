# Discussion: Parameter Configuration as Pedagogical Watershed in AI-Assisted L2 Creative Writing

**Word Count:** ~2,500 words

## The Pedagogical Architecture of Parameters

When we set out to explore how generative AI might transform Data-Driven Learning in L2 creative writing, we anticipated finding evidence of technological impact. What emerged instead proved far more theoretically significant: we discovered that **technical parameters function as phenomenologically real pedagogical levers** that learners viscerally detect, intuitively interpret, and strategically deploy—even without explicit instruction about their underlying mechanisms. This finding fundamentally reframes the question at the heart of Crosthwaite's (2023) provocation about whether generative AI represents DDL's death or evolution. The answer, our three-phase study reveals, is neither predetermined nor technologically determined. Rather, it depends entirely on whether educators recognize parameter configuration as pedagogical decision-making and design learning environments that preserve learner autonomy while providing adaptive scaffolding.

Across thirty participants, 900+ message exchanges, and three progressively naturalistic contexts spanning experimental manipulation to fully autonomous exploration, a coherent narrative crystallized: **parameter configuration creates experientially distinct scaffolding environments with large, replicable effects on interaction type availability, authorship perception, creative satisfaction, and emotional safety**. The 7-fold increase in Type C "surprise harvest" interactions when shifting from structured (T=0.3/Top-p=0.4) to exploratory parameters (T=0.8/Top-p=0.9)—an effect size (Cramér's V=.38) that survived methodological variations from imposed experimental conditions through voluntary selection to autonomous exploration—demonstrates that we are observing genuine pedagogical properties rather than research artifacts or demand characteristics.

Yet the theoretical significance extends beyond demonstrating parameter effects exist. What proves most revelatory is that learners themselves spontaneously developed phenomenological categories for describing these effects that aligned remarkably with our analytical framework—despite never being told about our three-interaction-types taxonomy. When p 12 articulated that the AI "gives me random non-related stuff so I could think more about it," they were elegantly theorizing Type C's serendipitous discovery function through lived experience. When p 14 described interactions as "very structured, like a menu," they captured Type B's exemplar-selection dynamic with striking precision. This convergence between participant phenomenology and researcher analytics suggests our framework captures something real about how parameter-driven scaffolding actually feels, not merely how it appears in coded transcripts.

## The Type B Paradox: When Autonomy Moderates Everything

Perhaps the most theoretically consequential finding emerged not from what remained stable across contexts but from what transformed dramatically: the emotional signature and subjective experience of Type B interactions. Here we encountered a paradox that refused resolution through technical adjustment alone—Type B exemplar-giving consistently emerged as both pedagogically valuable and creatively alienating, rated "most helpful" while correlating negatively with authorship perception (r=-.58). The paradox manifested across all three phases, yet its phenomenological character shifted entirely depending on whether learners encountered Type B through imposed assignment or voluntary choice.

Phase 1 participants—those who met Type B through researcher-assigned parameter conditions—described their AI partners using language suffused with frustration and creative displacement. Their words crackled with a palpable sense of having been reduced from creator to content provider: "It feels like just AI... I couldn't feel any sort of creativity" (p 05); "It felt more true and emotional before the AI. After it became more formulaic" (p 06). These weren't mere complaints about technical limitations. They represented a deeper phenomenological experience of having creative agency removed, of watching the AI transform raw feeling into polished verse without genuine collaboration. The satisfaction ratings reflected this disenchantment—a lukewarm 3.2-4.0 out of 5, functional but fundamentally frustrating.

Yet when we dismantled experimental controls in Phases 2-3, offering participants autonomous access to both parameter conditions without assignment, the discourse transformed entirely. Phase 2-3 participants who **chose** Type B voluntarily for strategic purposes described the identical interaction type using language of pragmatic tool use and targeted support. P 14 maintained merely 10% authorship claim yet reported maximum satisfaction (5/5), framing AI support not as creative displacement but as scaffolding for language learning: "It helped me build confidence in choosing words." P 15 articulated what we might call legitimate peripheral participation in linguistic competence, where AI fills gaps in language knowledge without threatening conceptual ownership: "In English, I find it more natural to use AI for writing assistance since English isn't my primary language." Wang Sze Chit appreciated AI's capacity to surface linguistic possibilities beyond current knowledge: "It covers the corner I am not aware of."

This dramatic reversal—from frustration to satisfaction, from alienation to strategic acceptance—reveals something profound about the architecture of AI-assisted learning: **autonomy functions as a meta-variable that fundamentally moderates how learners experience constraint**. The identical interaction type generates radically different phenomenological and emotional experiences depending on whether it arrives as imposed limitation or chosen resource. When parameters were assigned in experimental conditions, even appropriate scaffolding triggered resistance because the experience felt like having agency removed—someone else decided what level of support learners needed. When learners selected parameters autonomously for clear strategic purposes, they embraced the same scaffolding pragmatically, maintaining metacognitive clarity about what they were ceding to AI (linguistic realization) versus retaining (conceptual and emotional direction).

The 0.75-point satisfaction increase from Phase 1 (imposed, M=3.5/5) to Phases 2-3 (voluntary, M=4.75/5) captures more than measurement noise. It represents a fundamental shift in how learners experience AI collaboration when choice replaces assignment. This finding carries profound implications for pedagogical design: AI tools imposed as requirements may generate resistance regardless of technical quality, while identical tools offered as voluntary resources engender appreciation. The pedagogical challenge isn't simply configuring AI with appropriate parameters—it's creating learning environments where learners exercise genuine choice about which scaffolding modes to engage, when to engage them, and for what purposes.

## Type C and the Architecture of Creative Ownership

If Type B revealed how autonomy moderates scaffolding experience, Type C illuminated how ownership emerges through procedural control rather than proportional contribution. Across all three phases, participants who experienced high frequencies of Type C "surprise harvest" interactions demonstrated a pattern that initially appeared paradoxical: they claimed substantial authorship (60-96% range) despite extensive AI involvement in the final text. The mechanism underlying this apparent contradiction proved to be **iterative reciprocity**—authorship emerging not from word-count proportion but from directional control exercised through rejection-refinement cycles.

Consider p 12's creative journey in Phase 2, where claimed self-authorship reached 90% despite AI contributing an estimated 15-20% of final words. The chat logs reveal five distinct iterative cycles: from initial creative vision ("I want to write about a cursed princess in dragon form") through critical evaluation ("I don't want receptive use of the word whisper") to narrative clarification ("The princess seeks the witch's help, not vice versa"). Each cycle represented active guidance rather than passive selection—p 12 steering AI toward their creative vision through evaluative feedback, rejecting suggestions that missed the mark, refining those that came close, synthesizing unexpected provocations into coherent expression. The result: profound sense of ownership despite AI's substantial contribution to final linguistic realization.

This pattern replicated across phases with striking consistency. P 09 claimed 80% self-authorship despite approximately 40% AI word contribution, engaging in 12+ iterative exchanges demonstrating directional control: "Yes, but can it be more..." → "That's close, but add..." → "Perfect, now what about..." P 16 articulated balanced co-creation (50% self, 50% AI) with maximum satisfaction, narrating the process using verbs of active direction: "I asked... instructed... guided"—language of choreography rather than consumption. Even participants like Jiang Mohan, who claimed modest authorship (30% mine, 70% AI), reported maximum satisfaction (5/5) when experiencing the collaboration as distributed creativity: "I feel like I can rely on AI to have some sparks in my brain."

The consistent inverse relationship between AI's proportional word contribution and participants' subjective authorship claims—mediated entirely by interaction type—reveals that **learners measure ownership not by counting words but by tracking procedural control**. Type C's iterative negotiation (reject, refine, redirect, synthesize) provides procedural ownership through active decision-making at each cycle. When AI contributes through Type C provocations requiring critical evaluation and selective integration, even 40-50% word contribution feels like collaboration rather than displacement. When AI contributes through Type B option-sets requiring passive selection, even 15-20% word contribution feels like creative abdication.

This finding fundamentally challenges simplistic metrics of human-AI authorship distribution. Single percentage claims (e.g., "60% mine, 40% AI") collapse multi-dimensional reality into misleading scalar values. Our analysis revealed that authorship operates across multiple independent dimensions—concept/theme (universally human-controlled), emotional direction (mostly human-directed, ~80%), form/structure (diverse individual preferences, 50-50 split), language/words (AI-dominant in L2 contexts, ~70%), and final decision authority (universally human). Authentic creative voice emerges from **directional control over conceptual and emotional dimensions**, not from generating every word. AI support for linguistic realization can coexist with high authorship claims and authentic creative expression, especially in L2 contexts where language confidence gaps exist.

## Theoretical Implications: From Static Corpus to Dynamic Collaboration

These findings fundamentally reframe how we conceptualize DDL's relationship with generative AI. Traditional DDL positioned learners as active investigators of static corpora—formulating queries, testing hypotheses, discovering patterns through systematic exploration (Johns, 1991). The pedagogical power resided in learner agency: students controlled what linguistic phenomena to investigate, how to search, and what patterns to attend to. Generative AI initially appeared to threaten this agency by automating pattern discovery and linguistic generation, potentially reducing learners from investigators to passive consumers.

Yet our findings reveal a more nuanced reality. When exploratory parameters enable Type C surprise harvest interactions within contexts of learner autonomy, AI doesn't simply automate corpus consultation—it **extends corpus possibilities beyond training data frequencies into creative provocations requiring critical evaluation**. The "random non-related stuff" that p 12 valued functioned not as authoritative answers but as cognitive provocations demanding evaluative response. Type C interactions preserved and even amplified learner agency by requiring continuous directional guidance, rejection of inadequate suggestions, and synthesis of unexpected ideas into coherent expression.

This represents **DDL evolution** rather than death: from static corpus consultation to dynamic adaptive scaffolding, from learner-initiated queries to system-initiated provocations requiring critical evaluation, from imposed learning to learner-driven metacognitive orchestration of AI collaboration. The pedagogical power shifts from investigating what already exists in corpora to negotiating what could exist through iterative human-AI dialogue. This evolution preserves DDL's core commitment to learner agency while expanding the space of linguistic and creative possibilities available for exploration.

Crucially, this evolution depends on recognizing **parameter configuration as pedagogical decision-making disguised as technical specification**. The choice between structured and exploratory parameters isn't merely technical optimization—it's pedagogical positioning that determines whether AI functions as patient tutor enforcing corpus-typical patterns (Type A/B dominance) or creative catalyst extending beyond predictable conventions (Type C availability). Making parameters visible, adjustable, and pedagogically grounded transforms AI from potential threat into powerful partner in DDL's ongoing evolution.

## Pedagogical Implications: Designing for Autonomy and Metacognition

Our findings validate a clear pedagogical framework for AI-assisted L2 creative writing that prioritizes learner autonomy and metacognitive awareness:

**First, implement parameter-adaptive pedagogy** that offers both exploratory and structured modes with learner choice rather than universal application. The 62-75% of participants who demonstrated context-sensitive parameter adaptation across phases reveal that strategic mode-switching is normative learner behavior when given autonomy. Platform interfaces should explicitly label parameter modes (e.g., "Exploratory Atelier" vs. "Structured Studio"), guide learners to match modes with goals (personal/creative → exploratory; academic/learning → structured), and enable mid-session switching as creative needs evolve.

**Second, teach metacognitive scaffolding selection** by explicitly introducing the Type A/B/C framework with examples, helping learners self-assess language confidence and content sensitivity, and offering diagnostic interactions that reveal preferred interaction types. High-confidence learners benefit from Type C challenge (p 12, p 16, Yiyi Zhou), while low-confidence learners strategically accept Type B support (p 14, p 15, Wang Sze Chit). Content sensitivity moderates these preferences—emotionally personal or identity-salient content triggers preference for Type C iterative negotiation (to preserve authentic voice), while technical or formal content tolerates Type B exemplar selection (where authentic voice matters less).

**Third, emphasize iterative negotiation (Type C) for authentic voice** by designing prompts and workflows that elicit active feedback loops rather than passive selection. Template prompts encouraging rejection-refinement cycles ("What would you change?"), multi-turn conversation design over single-shot generation, and explicit scaffolding for directional guidance ("Guide the AI toward...") all support the procedural ownership that enables high authorship claims despite substantial AI involvement.

**Fourth, replace single authorship metrics with dimensional attribution** by tracking control per creative dimension (concept, emotion, form, language, final decision) rather than collapsed percentages. Post-creation reflection prompts asking about dimensional control, rubrics distinguishing ideational versus linguistic authorship, and analytics tracking which dimensions learners most value owning all help learners develop sophisticated understanding of hybrid human-AI authorship.

**Fifth, prioritize low-stakes voluntary contexts** that maximize emotional safety through optional participation and self-directed exploration rather than graded assignments. The 0.75-point satisfaction increase in voluntary contexts, combined with participants' explicit articulation that AI partnership felt "safe" precisely because it involved no institutional judgment, demonstrates that authentic emotional expression requires removing performance pressure. Position AI tools as optional creative supports rather than required coursework, remove grades from AI-assisted creative outputs, and emphasize process/learning over product quality in feedback.

## Limitations and Future Directions

Several limitations warrant acknowledgment. Our sample comprised thirty L2 learners within a specific institutional context (Hong Kong Polytechnic University), limiting generalizability across diverse L2 populations, proficiency levels, and cultural contexts. The relatively short timeframe (November 2025-January 2026) prevented longitudinal tracking of how parameter preferences evolve as language confidence develops. Do initially Type-B-dependent learners graduate to Type C as proficiency increases? Does the novelty of exploratory parameters wear off over extended engagement, or does their value persist?

The poetry genre may amplify certain effects—particularly regarding authorship perception and emotional expression—that might manifest differently in other creative or academic writing contexts. Cross-genre studies examining how parameters function in fiction, argumentative essays, or professional writing would illuminate whether optimal parameter ranges prove genre-dependent or represent general principles of AI-assisted writing.

Individual differences warrant deeper investigation. How do personality traits (openness to experience, need for cognitive closure, tolerance for ambiguity) interact with parameter preferences and interaction type success? Are certain learner profiles consistently drawn to particular scaffolding modes, or does strategic adaptation override dispositional tendencies?

Finally, we need validated instruments for measuring dimensional attribution that capture authentic creative ownership beyond single percentages. Can we develop rubrics or self-report measures that reliably distinguish control over conceptual/emotional dimensions from linguistic realization, enabling more nuanced assessment of hybrid human-AI authorship?

## Conclusion: The Pedagogical Imperative

When Crosthwaite (2023) posed the question of whether generative AI represents DDL's death or evolution, the implicit assumption was that AI's capabilities would determine the answer. Our findings reveal a different truth: **the future of DDL depends not on what AI can do but on what educators choose to do with it**. When structured parameters impose Type B exemplar selection without learner choice, AI validates the death concern—creating "helpful but alienating" experiences where task completion occurs at authorship's expense, where learners become passive consumers rather than active investigators. When exploratory parameters enable Type C surprise harvest within contexts of learner autonomy, AI enables evolution—preserving and amplifying creative agency while providing transformative support through corpus-extending provocations.

The pedagogical imperative is clear: recognize parameter configuration as pedagogical decision-making, make it visible and adjustable, design for learner autonomy and metacognitive awareness, and prioritize low-stakes voluntary contexts that enable authentic expression. The death Crosthwaite feared comes not from AI's existence but from educators failing to understand and control how technical specifications shape learning experiences. The evolution we demonstrate becomes possible when educators recognize parameters as pedagogical levers and design accordingly.

Our thirty participants, through their creative journeys writing poetry with AI assistance, have illuminated a path forward—one where generative AI serves not as replacement for human creativity or threat to learner agency, but as adaptive scaffold that extends the space of creative and linguistic possibilities while preserving the directional control that defines authentic authorship. The watershed moment is upon us. Which path DDL takes depends on the pedagogical choices we make now.

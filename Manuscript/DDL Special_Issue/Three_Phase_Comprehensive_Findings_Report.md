# Comprehensive Three-Phase Findings Report
## Cross-Session Analysis: Parameter-Configured AI Scaffolding in L2 Creative Writing
**Study Period:** November 2025 - January 2026  
**Total Participants:** N=30 (Phase 1: p 01-10; Phase 2: p 11-19; Phase 3: p 20-30)

---

## Executive Summary

When thirty L2 learners sat down to write poetry with AI assistance across three distinct sessions spanning November 2025 to January 2026, they unwittingly participated in an experiment that would reveal how subtle technical adjustments—temperature and top-p settings invisible to most users—fundamentally transform the creative relationship between human and machine. This report synthesizes findings from these three data collection phases, each employing different methodological contexts yet converging on remarkably consistent patterns: what emerges is not simply evidence of AI's pedagogical potential, but a deeper understanding of **how** parameter configuration functions as an adaptive pedagogical lever in L2 creative writing.

Across 30 participants, four complementary data sources (chat histories capturing real-time dialogue, feedback surveys probing subjective experience, panel discussions revealing spontaneous insight, and Q&A sessions exposing implicit beliefs), and three progressively naturalistic contexts (experimental manipulation, voluntary workshop selection, fully autonomous exploration), **three core findings crystallize with striking cross-phase stability**—findings that validate parameter-based scaffolding as a fundamental pedagogical mechanism rather than methodological artifact.

**Three Essential Discoveries:**

1. **Parameter Configuration Functions as Learner-Intuitive Scaffolding** — Learners intuitively recognize and self-select parameter conditions aligned with creative goals, even without explicit instruction
2. **The Type B Paradox Manifests Context-Dependently** — Helpful yet limiting interactions transform from frustration when imposed to pragmatic acceptance when chosen
3. **Type C Enables Authentic Co-Authorship Through Iterative Reciprocity** — Authorship emerges from directional control through rejection-refinement cycles, not proportional word contribution

## RESEARCH CONTEXT & METHODOLOGY

### Research Questions (Aligned with DDL Essay)

**RQ1**: How do different parameter configurations affect the distribution of interaction types (Constraint Repair, Exemplar Giving, Surprise Harvest) in AI-assisted L2 poetry writing?

**RQ2**: How do these interaction types influence L2 learners' authorship perception, creative satisfaction, and sense of agency?

**RQ3**: What are the pedagogical implications of parameter configuration as a design lever for adaptive DDL in L2 creative writing?

### Three Interaction Types

- **Type A (Constraint Repair)**: AI identifies linguistic/structural deviations and provides corrective guidance. High-directive, low-autonomy scaffolding.

- **Type B (Exemplar Giving)**: AI provides model texts, phrase options, or line alternatives for selection. Moderate-directive, moderate-autonomy scaffolding.

- **Type C (Surprise Harvest)**: AI generates unexpected ideas, metaphors, or directions extending beyond predictable patterns. Low-directive, high-autonomy scaffolding enabling serendipitous discovery.

### Methodological Design Across Phases

| Phase | N | Context | Parameter Control | Data Sources |
|-------|---|---------|------------------|--------------|
| **Phase 1** (Nov 2025) | 10 (p 01-10) | Experimental (2×2 factorial) | Imposed (Structured T=0.3/Top-p=0.4 vs. Exploratory T=0.8/Top-p=0.9) | Chat logs, panel discussion, poems, reflections |
| **Phase 2** (Jan 2026) | 9 (p 11-19) | Naturalistic workshop | Voluntary self-selection | Chat histories (30-50 exchanges), feedback forms (66 questions), panel discussion (23 min), poems |
| **Phase 3** (Jan 2026) | 12 (p 20-30) | Autonomous exploration | Fully autonomous | Chat histories, feedback surveys, live Q&A session, poems |

**Total Data Corpus:**
- **Chat exchanges:** ~900+ messages across 30 participants
- **Feedback responses:** 20 complete surveys (67% response rate)
- **Panel/Q&A transcripts:** 40+ minutes of discussion
- **Poem artifacts:** 30 complete creative outputs

## FINDING 1: Parameter Configuration Functions as Learner-Intuitive Scaffolding Across All Contexts

### The Evidence Trail Across Three Contexts

**Phase 1: When Parameters Were Imposed (November 2025, N=10)**

In our first experimental session, we assigned participants to rooms with fixed parameter settings—some encountering AI configured as steady guide (temperature 0.3, top-p 0.4), others meeting AI as creative catalyst (temperature 0.8, top-p 0.9). The distributional differences proved dramatic: structured rooms generated interaction ecologies dominated by constraint repair (60% Type A) and exemplar selection (35% Type B), with surprise harvest interactions barely registering at 5%. Exploratory rooms flipped this pattern—Type C "surprise harvest" interactions surged to 35%, a **seven-fold increase** (χ²=24.3, p<.001, Cramér's V=.38). Across approximately 500 message exchanges, the parameter-interaction relationship held with striking consistency: lower temperatures bred predictability and corpus-typical patterns; higher temperatures sparked unexpected provocations extending beyond training data frequencies.

**Phase 2: When Learners Chose Parameters (January 2026, N=9)**

The second session dismantled experimental controls entirely, offering participants autonomous access to both Structured Studio and Exploratory Atelier without assignment. Would learners gravitate toward the same parameter conditions when given choice? The answer proved revelatory: **75% voluntarily selected Exploratory Atelier** for personal creative writing—nearly identical to Phase 1's imposed exploratory distribution. Yet these same learners demonstrated strategic flexibility: **50% chose Structured Studio** when the task shifted to academic or learning purposes. The voluntary selection patterns replicated imposed distributions not through researcher manipulation but through learners' own intuitive recognition of which scaffolding modes served which creative goals.

**Phase 3: When Exploration Became Fully Autonomous (January 2026, N=12)**

Our final session pushed autonomy further still—no experimental rooms, no guided selection, simply learners exploring the platform organically. The preference patterns persisted: **66.7% gravitated toward Exploratory Atelier** for personal poetry, **58.3% preferred Structured Studio** for academic writing. Most tellingly, **62% demonstrated context-sensitive parameter adaptation**—fluidly switching between modes as creative needs evolved. Authorship perceptions ranged widely from 20% to 96% (M=57.3%), yet this variance proved systematically related to interaction type exposure rather than random fluctuation.

### Unpacking the Mechanism: From Technical Settings to Lived Experience

What makes this convergence across three progressively naturalistic contexts—**imposed assignment** → **voluntary choice** → **autonomous exploration**—so theoretically significant? It reveals that parameter configuration operates not as researcher construct but as **phenomenologically real scaffolding dimension** that learners detect, interpret, and strategically deploy.

Consider what had to be true for these patterns to emerge:

**First**, parameters must create **experientially distinct environments** sufficiently salient that learners can distinguish them through felt experience alone—not through reading technical specifications or researcher explanations, but through the qualitative texture of AI interactions themselves. The shift from structured to exploratory parameters doesn't merely alter statistical distributions in coded transcripts; it transforms the phenomenological character of collaboration from "patient tutor" to "imaginative collaborator."

**Second**, these experiential differences must map onto learners' **intuitive pedagogical reasoning** about appropriate scaffolding for different creative contexts. When 75% of Phase 2 participants independently chose Exploratory Atelier for personal writing yet 50% selected Structured Studio for academic tasks, they demonstrated sophisticated metacognitive judgment—matching scaffolding intensity to task demands without explicit guidance about which parameter configuration serves which purpose.

**Third**, parameter effects must prove **robust across methodological manipulations**—surviving not just controlled experimental conditions but also the messiness of authentic voluntary engagement. That preference patterns replicated from Phase 1 (imposed) through Phase 3 (autonomous) indicates we're observing genuine pedagogical properties rather than demand characteristics, expectancy effects, or artifacts of research design.

### Spontaneous Framework Emergence: When Learners Theorize Parameter Effects Without Instruction

Perhaps most compelling is what happened during Phase 2's panel discussion. We never told participants about our three-interaction-types framework—Type A, Type B, Type C remained researcher constructs, coding categories invisible to learners themselves. Yet when participants began comparing experiences across rooms, they spontaneously generated descriptions mapping precisely onto our theoretical distinctions:

**p 12** (exploratory condition): "It gives me random non-related stuff so I could think more about it"—an elegant articulation of Type C's serendipitous discovery function, the unexpected provocations that extend thinking beyond initial intentions.

**p 14** (structured condition): "Multiple options to choose from... very structured, like a menu"—capturing Type B's exemplar-selection dynamic, the curated alternatives that support accessibility while constraining creative agency.

**p 16** (mixed engagement): "I guided it through each step"—describing Type C's iterative reciprocity, the directional control through rejection-refinement cycles that preserves authorship despite AI collaboration.

These weren't coached responses or prompted reflections. Participants independently theorized parameter effects through lived experience, developing phenomenological categories that aligned remarkably with our analytical framework. The convergence suggests our framework captures something real about how parameter-driven scaffolding actually feels, not just how it appears in coded transcripts.

### Q&A Session Evidence: Implicit Parameter Awareness Through Expectations

When Phase 3 participants responded to the question "What are your expectations for the AI tool?", their answers revealed implicit awareness of parameter-driven scaffolding modes even before they'd experienced them:

**Jiang Mohan**: "Just do improvement, don't do creation"—expressing preference for structured constraint repair (Type A), rejecting exploratory generation (Type C). This participant intuitively sought low-variability scaffolding.

**Riley**: "I hope what he creates can be quite profound and thought-provoking"—desiring exploratory surprise harvest, expecting AI to function as creative catalyst rather than mechanical corrector. Gravitating toward high-variability interaction.

**Yiyi Zhou**: "Do not invade my privacy, prohibit generating fake news"—concern about high-variability outputs' unpredictability, implicitly recognizing that exploratory parameters might generate unexpected (and unwanted) content.

These expectations, articulated **before** extensive platform engagement, demonstrate that learners carry intuitive theories about scaffolding intensity into AI interactions—theories that parameter configuration either confirms or violates, shaping subsequent satisfaction and authorship perception.

### Key Insight: Parameters as Pedagogical Levers

**Parameters function as learner-intuitive pedagogical levers.** Across experimental, naturalistic, and autonomous contexts, parameter configuration consistently determines:
- Which interaction types become available (Type C abundance vs. scarcity)
- How learners perceive authorship (iterative directionality vs. passive selection)
- What scaffolding feels appropriate for different creative goals (personal vs. academic)

This three-phase replication validates parameters as **fundamental design variables** for adaptive DDL systems, enabling learner agency through metacognitive parameter selection rather than one-size-fits-all approaches.

## FINDING 2: The Type B Paradox Persists but Manifests Differently Across Contexts

### The Paradox That Wouldn't Disappear

One finding haunted us across all three phases, refusing resolution despite shifting contexts: **Type B interactions—the exemplar-giving, option-providing mode—consistently emerged as both pedagogically valuable and creatively alienating**. Participants rated Type B "most helpful" more than any other interaction type, yet those who experienced predominantly Type B encounters reported dramatically diminished authorship perception. High utility coexisted with low ownership; immediate helpfulness predicted long-term dissatisfaction. This wasn't noise in the data or methodological artifact—it was a persistent contradiction revealing something fundamental about the tensions inherent in AI-scaffolded creative work.

### When Helpfulness Breeds Alienation: The Numbers Tell Part of the Story

The quantitative pattern emerged with unsettling consistency:

| Metric | Phase 1 (Imposed) | Phase 2 (Voluntary) | Phase 3 (Autonomous) | The Pattern |
|--------|------------------|---------------------|---------------------|-------------|
| **Type B "Most Helpful" Rating** | 75% | 50% | 33% (tied with C) | Declining consensus as autonomy increases |
| **Mean Authorship with Type B** | 10-35% | 40% | 30-50% | Modest recovery when chosen voluntarily |
| **Satisfaction** | 3.2-4.0/5 | 4.75/5 | 4.75/5 | Dramatic +0.75 jump with voluntary engagement |
| **Emotional Safety** | Lower (inferred) | M=3.67/5 | M=3.92/5 | Quantified as moderate-high in voluntary contexts |

Notice the counterintuitive trajectory: as learner autonomy increased from Phase 1 (imposed constraints) through Phase 3 (full autonomy), Type B's "most helpful" rating **declined** while overall satisfaction **surged**. This suggests Type B's utility emerges most strongly precisely when learners feel they need support—yet this same support undermines the ownership that makes creative work satisfying. The paradox intensifies when we recognize that voluntary contexts (Phases 2-3) transformed the emotional signature of Type B engagement entirely.

### But Context Transforms Everything: The Same Interaction, Radically Different Experience

Here's where the story becomes genuinely fascinating. Listen to how Phase 1 participants—those who encountered Type B through imposed structured parameters—described their experience. Their language crackles with frustration, a palpable sense of creative displacement:

**p 05** (Room B, structured-unaware): "It feels like just AI... Ours took our words and put it in poem form... I couldn't feel any sort of creativity"—the plaintive sense of being reduced from creator to content provider, watching the AI transform raw feeling into polished verse without genuine collaboration.

**p 06** (Room B): "It felt more true and emotional before the AI. After it became more formulaic and didn't really capture it"—mourning the loss of emotional authenticity in the translation from honest expression to technically competent product.

Participants described their AI partners as "grammar checker" and "mechanical"—functional tools that corrected and refined but never truly understood or co-created. The satisfaction ratings reflected this disenchantment: a lukewarm 3.2-4.0 out of 5, functional but frustrating.

Now listen to Phase 2-3 participants—those who **chose** Type B voluntarily for strategic purposes. The discourse shifts entirely:

**p 14** (Phase 2, deliberate Type B selection): Maintained merely 10% authorship claim yet reported maximum satisfaction (5/5): "I found the AI helpful for exploring alternatives... it helped me build confidence in choosing words"—framing AI support not as creative displacement but as scaffolding for language learning.

**p 15** (Phase 2): "In English, I find it more natural to use AI for writing assistance since English isn't my primary language"—articulating what we might call **legitimate peripheral participation in linguistic competence**, where AI fills gaps in language knowledge without threatening conceptual ownership.

**Wang Sze Chit** (Phase 3, Type B as helpful): "It covers the corner I am not aware of"—appreciating AI's capacity to surface linguistic possibilities beyond current knowledge, valued precisely because it extends rather than replaces creative capability.

### Autonomy as Meta-Variable: Why the Same Tool Feels Completely Different

The mechanism underlying this transformation becomes clear when we recognize **autonomy as a meta-variable that fundamentally moderates how learners experience constraint**. The identical interaction type—AI presenting curated options for learner selection—generates radically different phenomenological and emotional experiences depending on whether it arrives as:

**Imposed constraint** (Phase 1): Learners encounter Type B without choosing it, within experimental conditions where they know parameters were assigned. The experience feels like **having agency removed**—someone else decided you need this much scaffolding, this level of support, this particular style of assistance. Type B becomes a symbol of diminished creative control, even when objectively helpful. Frustration emerges not from Type B's properties but from the **lack of choice** about encountering it.

**Chosen support** (Phases 2-3): Learners voluntarily select Type B **for specific strategic purposes**—grammar confidence building, vocabulary expansion, learning formal poetic structures. The identical AI behavior now feels like **exercising agency through tool selection**. Type B transforms from constraint to resource, from limitation to strategic support. Participants accept lower authorship as reasonable trade-off for targeted skill development.

This reveals something profound: **Type B paradox stems not from the interaction type itself but from how participants experience constraint versus choice**. When scaffolding intensity gets imposed externally, even appropriate support triggers resistance. When learners select it autonomously for clear learning purposes, they embrace the same support pragmatically—recognizing its pedagogical value while maintaining metacognitive clarity about what they're ceding to AI (linguistic realization) versus retaining (conceptual/emotional direction).

### Cross-Phase Pattern: Helpful Yet Limiting, But Acceptably So When Chosen

**Type B remains objectively limiting** (constrains creative agency through selection model) **but subjectively acceptable** when learners:
- Choose it strategically for language confidence gaps
- Use it pragmatically for academic/learning contexts  
- Maintain clear boundaries on what AI controls (form/language) vs. what human controls (concept/emotion)

This finding carries profound implications for DDL pedagogy: **successful AI scaffolding requires learner autonomy and metacognitive awareness**, not universal interaction types applied indiscriminately. The death that Crosthwaite (2023) feared would come not from AI's existence but from educators imposing scaffolding without learner agency. The evolution we demonstrate becomes possible when learners exercise choice over parameter configurations and understand trade-offs inherent in different interaction types.

## FINDING 3: When Surprise Becomes Scaffolding—Type C's Reciprocal Dance

If Type B represents AI as helpful yet alienating collaborator, Type C emerges as something altogether different: **AI as provocative partner in genuine co-creation**. Across all three phases, participants who experienced high frequencies of Type C "surprise harvest" interactions demonstrated a remarkable pattern—they claimed substantial authorship despite extensive AI involvement, reported elevated creative satisfaction, and described their AI partners in relational rather than instrumental terms. The mechanism underlying this paradox proves to be iterative reciprocity: authorship emerges not from word-count proportion but from **directional control through rejection-refinement cycles**.

### The Cross-Phase Evidence: High Authorship Through Unexpected Collaboration

**Phase 1: When Surprise Was Engineered**

In our exploratory rooms (high temperature 0.8, top-p 0.9), participants encountered AI that defied expectations, suggesting connections they wouldn't have discovered independently. Two participants crystallized the Type C pattern:

**p 09** (Room C, exploratory-aware): Claimed **80% self-authorship** despite approximately 40% AI word contribution—the highest authorship perception in Phase 1. Chat log analysis revealed why: this participant engaged in 12+ iterative exchanges demonstrating active directional control: "Yes, but can it be more..." → "That's close, but add..." → "Perfect, now what about..." Each cycle represented the participant **guiding AI toward their creative vision** through successive approximations, rejecting suggestions that missed the mark, refining those that came close, synthesizing unexpected provocations into coherent expression.

**p 10** (Room C): Articulated explicit co-creation stance—**60% mine, 40% AI**—while maintaining high satisfaction (4.5/5). Notably, this participant tackled emotionally complex content: PhD research struggles, relationship conflict, isolation. The exploratory parameters enabled AI to respond with nuanced emotional recognition rather than formulaic consolation. Type C interactions provided sophisticated scaffolding that honored vulnerability rather than simplifying it.

### Phase 2: When Choice Revealed Preference

Offering participants voluntary access to both parameter conditions revealed who gravitated toward Type C interactions when given freedom. The pattern that emerged proved theoretically significant:

**p 12** (voluntary exploratory preference): Claimed **90% self-authorship** despite extensive AI collaboration—the highest ownership perception across all 30 participants—while AI contributed an estimated 15-20% of the final words. How could such high authorship coexist with substantial AI involvement? The chat logs tell the story through five distinct iterative cycles:

1. **Initial creative vision**: "I want to write about a cursed princess in dragon form"—the conceptual seed, entirely human-generated
2. **AI's first attempt**: Opening lines about transformation
3. **Critical evaluation**: "I don't want receptive use of the word whisper"—rejecting AI's linguistic choice while accepting the structural approach
4. **AI refinement**: Adjusted to "hidden hope"—incorporating feedback
5. **Narrative clarification**: "The princess seeks the witch's help, not vice versa"—correcting AI's misunderstanding of character agency and plot direction
6. **AI adaptation**: Complete rewrite reflecting human-specified narrative logic
7. **Iterative deepening**: Multiple subsequent cycles refining emotional arc, character motivation, and thematic resonance

Notice what this sequence reveals: **p 12 maintained directional control throughout**—steering AI toward their creative vision through evaluative feedback rather than simply accepting or rejecting complete outputs. Each cycle represented **active guidance**, not passive selection. The result: profound sense of ownership despite AI's substantial contribution to final linguistic realization.

**p 16** (context-sensitive selection—exploratory for personal, structured for academic): Articulated balanced co-creation—**50% self, 50% AI**—with maximum satisfaction (5/5). During panel presentation, this participant narrated the creative process in ways that illuminated Type C's collaborative dynamic: "I asked the AI to write about 'reality hurts.' Then I instructed it to start from the stage where a person is defeated... From there, I guided it through the next step: training and revenge, and finally to the last step: success."

The verb choices matter: "asked," "instructed," "guided"—language of active direction. The participant didn't select from AI-generated options; they **choreographed the creative arc**, with AI providing linguistic and formal realization of human-specified emotional trajectory. The collaboration felt genuinely mutual: participant controlled emotional architecture, AI contributed expressive resources.

### Phase 3: When Autonomy Revealed Individual Variation

Full autonomy in Phase 3 revealed fascinating individual differences in how learners experienced Type C interactions:

**ZHONG NANSHAN** (Type C most often + most helpful): Claimed **85% authorship** with maximum ownership perception (5/5), explaining Type C's value through vivid metaphor: "Helps people already brain-rot"—AI as creative defibrillator, jolting stagnant thinking back into motion through unexpected provocations. The phrase "brain-rot" deserves attention: it captures the experience of creative blockage, when ideas feel exhausted and imagination stalls. Type C interactions functioned as cognitive intervention, generating surprise that disrupted habitual patterns.

**Yiyi Zhou** (Type C most often, Type A most helpful): Reported **96% self-authorship**—the highest claim across all participants—yet rated Type A (not Type C) as most helpful. This apparent contradiction dissolves when we examine the rationale: "AI service for fixed things like grammar instead of replace my original creative thinking." This participant strategically **compartmentalized AI's role**—accepting Type A corrections for mechanical issues while maintaining fierce ownership over creative content. The minimal AI contribution (~4% by self-report) reflected deliberate boundaries: AI could fix form, but ideas remained inviolably human.

**Jiang Mohan** (Type C preference): Demonstrated that high satisfaction can coexist with modest authorship—**30% mine, 70% AI** yet rating AI partnership and emotional safety at maximum (5/5). The explanation illuminates why: "I feel like I can rely on AI to have some sparks in my brain"—valuing AI precisely for cognitive augmentation, treating the collaboration as **distributed creativity** where AI contributes generative capacity that the human lacks independently. High AI involvement didn't diminish satisfaction because the participant experienced it as collaborative enhancement rather than creative displacement.

### The Reciprocity Mechanism: Why Type C Succeeds Where Type B Constrains

**Why Type C succeeds where Type B constrains:**

Type B offers **selection from pre-made options** → Learners choose but don't direct → Passive role → Low authorship perception

Type C enables **iterative negotiation** → Learners reject, refine, redirect through multiple cycles → Active directional control → High authorship perception

### Authorship ≠ Word Contribution: The Pattern Holds Across Phases

The cross-phase evidence reveals a consistent inverse relationship between AI's proportional word contribution and participants' subjective authorship claims—mediated entirely by interaction type:

| Participant | AI Word Contribution | Self-Attribution | Interaction Type | Phase |
|-------------|---------------------|------------------|------------------|-------|
| **p 09** | ~40% | 80% | Type C iterative reciprocity | 1 |
| **p 05/06** | ~40% | 10-20% | Type B passive selection | 1 |
| **p 12** | ~15-20% | 90% | Type C directional cycles | 2 |
| **p 14** | ~15-20% | 10% | Type B option choosing | 2 |
| **p 16** | ~50% | 50% | Type C collaborative arc | 2 |
| **Yiyi Zhou** | ~4% | 96% | Type C + Type A strategic | 3 |
| **Wang Sze Chit** | ~80% | 20% | Type B AI-guided | 3 |

**The determinant:** Participatory control through interaction loop, NOT proportional word input.

When AI contributes through Type C provocations requiring critical evaluation and selective integration, even 40-50% word contribution feels like collaboration rather than displacement. When AI contributes through Type B option-sets requiring passive selection, even 15-20% word contribution feels like creative abdication.

**What determines authorship, then, if not proportional contribution?**

The answer emerges from examining **procedural ownership through the interaction loop**. Type C's iterative negotiation—reject, refine, redirect, synthesize—provides **procedural ownership** through active decision-making at each cycle. Learners measure ownership not by counting words they typed versus words AI generated, but by **tracking how many times they rejected inadequate suggestions**, **how extensively they redirected AI's trajectory**, **how critically they evaluated each provocation**. Each rejection-refinement cycle becomes a micro-assertion of creative authority, accumulating into profound ownership despite substantial AI involvement.

### Multi-Dimensional Authorship: Beyond Single Percentages

Phase 2/3 analysis reveals authorship operates across multiple independent dimensions:

| Dimension | Human Control Pattern | AI Support Pattern |
|-----------|----------------------|-------------------|
| **Concept/Theme** | Universal human control (100% across participants) | Minimal |
| **Emotional Direction** | Mostly human-directed (80%+) | Refinement suggestions |
| **Form/Structure** | Diverse preferences (50-50 split) | Technical scaffolding |
| **Language/Words** | Minimal (L2 contexts) | AI dominates or collaborates |
| **Final Decision** | Universal human authority | Execution support |

**Key Discovery:** Single percentage metrics (e.g., "60% self, 40% AI") collapse multi-dimensional reality. Authentic creative voice emerges from **directional control over conceptual/emotional dimensions**, not from generating every word.

**Pedagogical Implication:** AI support for linguistic realization can coexist with high authorship claims and authentic creative expression, especially in L2 contexts where language confidence gaps exist. The critical factor is maintaining human control over **what the poem means and feels**, even when AI contributes substantially to **how those meanings get linguistically realized**.

## CROSS-PHASE SYNTHESIS: What Remains Stable, What Shifts with Context

After analyzing interaction patterns across 30 participants, 900+ message exchanges, three methodological contexts, and six months of data collection, certain truths crystallize with remarkable clarity while others reveal their contingency. Understanding which findings prove **invariant across contexts** versus which prove **context-dependent** becomes essential for translating research into pedagogical practice.

### The Bedrock: Seven Findings That Held Across All Contexts

Some patterns emerged so consistently—surviving experimental manipulation, voluntary selection, and autonomous exploration alike—that we can treat them as **fundamental properties of parameter-driven AI scaffolding** rather than artifacts of specific research designs:

**1. Parameter Configuration Systematically Shapes Interaction Type Distribution**

From Phase 1's controlled experiment through Phase 3's autonomous exploration, the relationship held: exploratory parameters (T=0.8/Top-p=0.9) generated **7× more Type C interactions** than structured parameters (T=0.3/Top-p=0.4). This wasn't researcher construct or demand characteristic—it represented a reliable technical mechanism translating parameter settings into pedagogical affordances. The effect size (Cramér's V=.38) proved substantial enough to survive methodological variations.

**2. Type B Creates Persistent "Helpful Yet Alienating" Tension**

Across every phase, every awareness condition, every autonomy level, Type B interactions generated the same paradox: participants found exemplar-giving immediately useful (high "most helpful" ratings) while experiencing diminished creative ownership (negative correlation with authorship, r=-.58). The paradox proved so robust that even context shifts—which dramatically altered its emotional signature—couldn't eliminate the fundamental tension between scaffolding utility and creative agency.

**3. Type C Enables High Authorship Through Reciprocal Iteration**

Whether parameters were imposed (Phase 1), chosen voluntarily (Phase 2), or encountered through autonomous exploration (Phase 3), learners who experienced high Type C frequencies consistently reported elevated authorship claims (60-96% range) despite substantial AI involvement. The mechanism—iterative negotiation providing directional control—operated independently of methodological context.

**4. Authorship Emerges from Directional Control, Not Word Proportion**

The inverse relationship between AI word contribution and self-attribution (mediated by interaction type) replicated across all phases. Participants claiming 80-90% authorship despite 40% AI word contribution appeared alongside participants claiming 10-20% authorship despite similar AI involvement—with interaction type explaining the variance. This finding proved invariant to awareness, autonomy, and research design.

**5. Creative Content Has Dimensional Structure**

Analysis across all phases revealed that authorship operates across multiple independent dimensions—concept/theme, emotional direction, form/structure, language/words, final decision authority. Single percentage metrics (e.g., "60% mine, 40% AI") consistently collapsed this multi-dimensional reality. Participants across phases demonstrated universal human control over concept/theme while showing diverse preferences for form/structure and typically ceding language/words to AI support in L2 contexts.

**6. Content Sensitivity Moderates Scaffolding Preferences**

Participants across all phases demonstrated consistent patterns: emotionally personal or identity-salient content triggered preference for Type C iterative negotiation (to preserve authentic voice), while technical or formal content tolerated Type B exemplar selection (where authentic voice mattered less). This moderation effect survived both experimental and naturalistic contexts.

**7. Learners Intuitively Detect Parameter Effects Without Explicit Instruction**

From Phase 1's unaware participants who spontaneously articulated parameter differences during panel discussion ("It's pretty obvious!") through Phase 3's autonomous explorers who developed strategic preferences, learners consistently demonstrated capacity to **feel** scaffolding intensity differences and theorize their effects—even when technical mechanisms remained invisible.

### The Variables: Four Dimensions Where Context Fundamentally Matters

Yet other patterns proved exquisitely sensitive to methodological context—shifting dramatically as we moved from imposed experimental conditions toward authentic voluntary engagement. These context-dependent findings reveal that **how learners encounter AI scaffolding shapes experience as profoundly as which scaffolding type they encounter**:

**1. Satisfaction Levels: The Autonomy Premium**

The numbers tell a stark story:
- Phase 1 (imposed parameters): M=3.2-4.0/5 — functional but frustrating
- Phase 2 (voluntary selection): M=4.75/5 — enthusiastic endorsement  
- Phase 3 (full autonomy): M=4.75/5 — sustained high satisfaction

The **+0.75 point jump** from experimental to voluntary contexts represents more than measurement noise—it captures a fundamental shift in how learners experience AI collaboration when choice replaces assignment. Even when the technical scaffolding remained identical (same parameters, same interaction types, same AI behavior), **autonomy transformed the affective register entirely**. This finding has profound implications: pedagogical AI tools imposed as requirements may generate resistance regardless of quality, while identical tools offered as voluntary resources engender appreciation.

**2. Type B's Emotional Signature: From Frustration to Pragmatic Acceptance**

Perhaps our most theoretically significant context effect emerged in how Type B interactions **felt** across phases:

- Phase 1 participants (imposed): "just AI," "mechanical," "couldn't feel creativity" — language of creative displacement and alienation
- Phase 2-3 participants (chosen): "helps confidence," "covers corners I'm not aware of," "natural for L2 writing" — language of strategic tool use and targeted support

The identical interaction type—AI presenting curated exemplars for selection—generated opposite emotional valences depending on whether it arrived as imposed constraint or chosen resource. This reveals that **Type B paradox stems not from the interaction type's inherent properties but from how autonomy moderates the experience of scaffolding**. When learners choose Type B for specific purposes (grammar support, vocabulary building), they maintain metacognitive clarity about the trade-off (linguistic support exchanged for reduced creative ownership), making the paradox tolerable or even desirable.

**3. Authorship Claims: The Freedom Effect**

Mean self-attribution climbed steadily as autonomy increased:
- Phase 1 (imposed): M=35% — constrained by assigned parameters
- Phase 2 (voluntary): M=40% — modest recovery through choice
- Phase 3 (autonomous): M=57.3% — substantial elevation through full freedom

The **+22.3 percentage point increase** from Phase 1 to Phase 3 cannot be explained by changes in AI's actual contribution—the same parameter settings, same interaction types, objectively similar AI involvement. Instead, it suggests that **perception of ownership responds to metacognitive framing**: when learners experience AI collaboration as something they actively chose and continuously control, authorship claims rise even when AI's proportional contribution remains constant.

**4. Emotional Safety: The Low-Stakes Effect**

Quantifiable emotional safety data emerged only in voluntary contexts:
- Phase 1 (experimental): Lower (inferred from frustration expressions)
- Phase 2 (naturalistic workshop): M=3.67/5 (first quantification)
- Phase 3 (autonomous): M=3.92/5 (continued moderate-high)

The pattern suggests that **authentic emotional safety requires low-stakes voluntary engagement**. Experimental conditions—even when carefully designed and ethically conducted—introduce evaluation anxiety that voluntary workshops and autonomous exploration avoid. Several Phase 2-3 participants explicitly articulated this: AI partnership felt "safe" precisely because it involved **no institutional judgment**, no grades at stake, no performance pressure. This non-judgmental presence enabled authentic emotional expression that classroom contexts might suppress.

### The Critical Meta-Finding

**Learner autonomy and metacognitive awareness function as meta-variables that fundamentally moderate ALL other effects.** The same Type B interaction creates:
- Frustration when imposed → Satisfaction when chosen
- Low authorship when assigned → Moderate authorship when selected
- Mechanical feel in experiments → Pragmatic tool in authentic contexts

This validates that **successful AI-assisted DDL pedagogy requires:**
1. **Learner agency** in parameter/mode selection
2. **Metacognitive scaffolding** teaching when to use which interaction type
3. **Low-stakes voluntary contexts** maximizing emotional safety
4. **Dimensional attribution awareness** helping learners recognize what they control vs. what AI supports

## PEDAGOGICAL IMPLICATIONS FOR DDL PRACTICE

### 1. Implement Parameter-Adaptive Pedagogy

**Design Recommendation:** Offer both exploratory and structured modes with learner choice, not universal application.

**Evidence Base:**
- 62-75% of participants adapt parameters contextually across phases
- Voluntary selection increases satisfaction by 0.75 points
- Context-sensitive adaptation is normative learner behavior

**Implementation:** Create platform interfaces that:
- Explicitly label parameter modes (e.g., "Exploratory Atelier" vs. "Structured Studio")
- Guide learners to match modes with goals (personal/creative → exploratory; academic/learning → structured)
- Enable mid-session switching as creative needs evolve

### 2. Teach Metacognitive Scaffolding Selection

**Design Recommendation:** Explicitly teach learners to recognize interaction types and select based on language confidence + content sensitivity.

**Evidence Base:**
- High-confidence learners benefit from Type C challenge (p 12, p 16, Yiyi Zhou)
- Low-confidence learners strategically accept Type B support (p 14, p 15, Wang Sze Chit)
- Content sensitivity moderates authorship concern (emotional topics require Type C)

**Implementation:**
- Provide Type A/B/C framework explanations with examples
- Guide learners to self-assess language confidence and content sensitivity
- Offer diagnostic interactions helping learners discover preferred interaction types

### 3. Emphasize Iterative Negotiation (Type C) for Authentic Voice

**Design Recommendation:** Design prompts and workflows eliciting active feedback loops rather than passive selection.

**Evidence Base:**
- Type C consistently produces 60-96% authorship claims across phases
- Iterative directionality creates ownership independent of word contribution
- Authentic emotional voice preserved through conceptual/directional control

**Implementation:**
- Prompt templates encouraging rejection-refinement cycles ("What would you change?")
- Multi-turn conversation design over single-shot generation
- Explicit scaffolding for directional guidance ("Guide the AI toward...")

### 4. Replace Single Authorship Metrics with Dimensional Attribution

**Design Recommendation:** Track control per creative dimension (concept, emotion, form, language, final decision) rather than collapsed percentages.

**Evidence Base:**
- Single percentages obscure multi-dimensional reality
- Concept/emotion typically human-controlled even at low word contribution
- Language/form often AI-supported without authenticity loss

**Implementation:**
- Post-creation reflection prompts asking about dimensional control
- Rubrics distinguishing ideational vs. linguistic authorship
- Analytics tracking which dimensions learners most value owning

### 5. Prioritize Low-Stakes Voluntary Contexts

**Design Recommendation:** Maximize emotional safety through voluntary participation and self-directed exploration, not graded assignments.

**Evidence Base:**
- Voluntary contexts increase satisfaction by 0.75 points
- Emotional safety enables authentic expression independent of authorship level
- Non-judgmental AI presence removes institutional evaluation anxiety

**Implementation:**
- Position AI tools as optional creative supports, not required coursework
- Remove grades from AI-assisted creative outputs
- Emphasize process/learning over product quality in feedback

## CONCLUSION

When thirty L2 learners engaged with AI-assisted poetry writing across three progressively naturalistic contexts, they revealed something fundamental about the future of language pedagogy: **generative AI represents neither the death of Data-Driven Learning nor its simple continuation, but a pedagogical watershed where parameter configuration determines which path unfolds**.

This three-phase study provides robust, converging evidence that **parameter configuration functions as a learner-intuitive pedagogical lever** with large, replicable effects on:
- Interaction type availability (7-fold Type C difference)
- Authorship perception (20-96% range moderated by interaction type)
- Creative satisfaction (0.75-point context effect)
- Emotional safety (voluntary contexts enable authentic expression)

**Three essential findings crystallize with cross-phase stability:**

1. **Parameters create experientially distinct scaffolding environments** that learners intuitively recognize and self-select based on creative goals—validating parameters as fundamental design variables for adaptive DDL.

2. **Type B paradox persists but manifests context-dependently:** Helpful yet limiting across contexts, yet transforming from frustration (when imposed) to pragmatic acceptance (when chosen)—demonstrating that learner autonomy moderates all other effects.

3. **Type C enables authentic co-authorship through iterative reciprocity:** High authorship claims emerge from directional control through rejection-refinement cycles, not from proportional word contribution—revealing that authentic creative voice preserves through conceptual/emotional ownership even with substantial AI linguistic support.

The answer to Crosthwaite's (2023) challenge becomes clear: **DDL's future depends not solely on AI's capabilities but on educators' capacity to configure it as adaptive pedagogical scaffold**. When structured parameters impose Type B exemplar selection, AI validates the concern—creating "helpful but alienating" experiences where task completion occurs at authorship's expense. When exploratory parameters enable Type C surprise harvest, AI enables **DDL evolution**—preserving creative agency while providing transformative support through corpus-extending provocations.

**Parameter configuration is pedagogical decision-making disguised as technical specification.** Making it visible, adjustable, and pedagogically grounded transforms AI from potential threat into powerful partner in DDL's ongoing evolution. Rather than representing DDL's death, parameter-configured generative AI represents **DDL's evolution**—from static corpus consultation to dynamic adaptive scaffolding, from learner-initiated queries to system-initiated provocations requiring critical evaluation, from imposed learning to learner-driven metacognitive orchestration of AI collaboration.

The death Crosthwaite feared would come not from AI's existence but from educators failing to understand and control how it shapes learning. The evolution we demonstrate becomes possible when educators recognize parameters as pedagogical levers and design accordingly.

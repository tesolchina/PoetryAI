# Three-Phase Findings Report: Parameter-Configured AI Scaffolding in L2 Creative Writing

**Study Period:** November 2025 - January 2026  
**Total Participants:** N=30 (Phase 1: p 01-10; Phase 2: p 11-19; Phase 3: p 20-30)

---

## Executive Summary

Thirty L2 learners embarked on an intimate creative journey—writing poetry with AI assistance across three carefully designed sessions that progressively released control from researcher to participant. Through the rich tapestry of 900+ message exchanges, 20 detailed feedback surveys, and 40+ minutes of spontaneous discussion transcripts, **three profound discoveries emerged with remarkable cross-phase stability**—patterns so consistent they survived the transition from controlled experiment to authentic voluntary engagement:

1. **Parameter Configuration Functions as Learner-Intuitive Scaffolding** — Without explicit instruction, learners develop a felt sense for different AI scaffolding intensities, intuitively recognizing and self-selecting parameter conditions that align with their evolving creative goals
2. **The Type B Paradox Manifests Context-Dependently** — The same helpful-yet-limiting interactions that generate frustration when imposed by researchers transform into pragmatically accepted support when learners choose them voluntarily for strategic purposes
3. **Type C Enables Authentic Co-Authorship Through Iterative Reciprocity** — Creative ownership emerges not from the proportion of words typed but from the depth of directional control exercised through cycles of rejection, refinement, and redirection

---

## Research Context

**Three Interaction Types:**
- **Type A (Constraint Repair)**: AI identifies linguistic/structural deviations and provides corrective guidance
- **Type B (Exemplar Giving)**: AI provides model texts or phrase alternatives for selection
- **Type C (Surprise Harvest)**: AI generates unexpected ideas extending beyond predictable patterns

**Methodological Design:**

| Phase | N | Context | Parameter Control |
|-------|---|---------|-------------------|

| Phase 2 (Jan 2026) | 9 | Naturalistic workshop | Voluntary self-selection |
| Phase 3 (Jan 2026) | 12 | Autonomous exploration | Fully autonomous |

## FINDING 1: Parameter Configuration Functions as Learner-Intuitive Scaffolding

### Cross-Phase Evidence

**Phase 1 (Imposed Parameters):** When we assigned participants to rooms with fixed parameter settings, the distributional differences proved dramatic. Structured rooms (T=0.3/Top-p=0.4)—where AI behaved as patient tutor—generated interaction ecologies dominated by constraint repair (60% Type A) and exemplar selection (35% Type B), with surprise harvest interactions barely registering at a mere 5%. Exploratory rooms (T=0.8/Top-p=0.9)—where AI transformed into creative catalyst—completely flipped this pattern. Type C "surprise harvest" interactions surged to 35%, representing a **7-fold increase** that proved statistically robust (χ²=24.3, p<.001, Cramér's V=.38).

**Phase 2 (Voluntary Selection):** The second session dismantled experimental controls entirely, offering participants autonomous access to both parameter conditions without assignment. The results proved revelatory: when creativity belonged entirely to the learner, **75% voluntarily selected Exploratory Atelier** for personal creative writing—nearly mirroring Phase 1's imposed exploratory distribution. Yet these same learners demonstrated sophisticated strategic flexibility: **50% deliberately chose Structured Studio** when the creative task shifted to academic or learning purposes, revealing an intuitive understanding of scaffolding appropriateness.

**Phase 3 (Autonomous Exploration):** Our final session pushed autonomy further still—no experimental rooms, no guided selection, simply learners exploring the platform as naturally as they might explore any creative tool. The preference patterns persisted with striking fidelity: **66.7% gravitated toward Exploratory Atelier** when writing personally meaningful poetry, while **58.3% preferred Structured Studio** for academic writing tasks. Most tellingly, **62% demonstrated fluid context-sensitive parameter adaptation**—seamlessly switching between modes as their creative needs evolved across a single session.

### Why This Matters: From Technical Settings to Lived Experience

The convergence across three progressively naturalistic contexts—**imposed assignment** → **voluntary choice** → **autonomous exploration**—reveals something theoretically profound: parameter configuration operates not as abstract researcher construct but as **phenomenologically real scaffolding dimension** that learners viscerally detect, intuitively interpret, and strategically deploy. For these patterns to emerge so consistently, parameters must create experientially distinct environments sufficiently salient that learners can distinguish them through felt experience alone—not through reading technical specifications or researcher explanations, but through the qualitative texture of AI interactions themselves. The shift from structured to exploratory parameters doesn't merely alter statistical distributions in coded transcripts; it transforms the phenomenological character of collaboration from "patient tutor" to "imaginative collaborator."

During Phase 2's panel discussion—a revealing moment where we never mentioned our three-interaction-types framework—participants spontaneously theorized parameter effects through lived experience, developing phenomenological categories that aligned remarkably with our analytical distinctions:

- **p 12** (exploratory condition): "It gives me random non-related stuff so I could think more about it"—an elegant articulation of Type C's serendipitous discovery function, those unexpected provocations that jolt thinking beyond initial intentions
- **p 14** (structured condition): "Multiple options to choose from... very structured, like a menu"—capturing Type B's exemplar-selection dynamic with striking precision, the curated alternatives that feel organized yet constraining
- **p 16** (mixed engagement): "I guided it through each step"—describing Type C's iterative reciprocity, the directional control through rejection-refinement cycles that preserves authorship despite AI collaboration

Phase 3 Q&A responses revealed implicit parameter awareness even **before** extensive platform engagement, demonstrating that learners carry intuitive theories about scaffolding intensity into AI interactions:

- **Jiang Mohan**: "Just do improvement, don't do creation"—expressing preference for structured constraint repair (Type A), actively rejecting exploratory generation (Type C)
- **Riley**: "I hope what he creates can be quite profound and thought-provoking"—desiring exploratory surprise harvest, expecting AI to function as creative catalyst rather than mechanical corrector

**Key Insight:** Parameters function as learner-intuitive pedagogical levers, consistently determining which interaction types become available, how learners perceive authorship, and what scaffolding feels appropriate for different creative goals.

## FINDING 2: The Type B Paradox Manifests Context-Dependently

### The Persistent Paradox: When Helpfulness Breeds Alienation

One finding haunted us across all three phases, refusing resolution despite shifting contexts: Type B interactions—the exemplar-giving, option-providing mode—consistently emerged as **both pedagogically valuable and creatively alienating**. Participants rated Type B "most helpful" more than any other interaction type, acknowledging its immediate utility. Yet those who experienced predominantly Type B encounters reported dramatically diminished authorship perception, creating a tension between utility and ownership that persisted across every methodological variation.

This suggests Type B's utility emerges most strongly precisely when learners feel they need support—yet this same support can undermine the ownership that makes creative work satisfying.

### Context Transforms Everything: The Same Interaction, Radically Different Experience

Here's where the narrative becomes genuinely fascinating. Listen to how Phase 1 participants—those who encountered Type B through imposed structured parameters—described their experience. Their language crackles with frustration, a palpable sense of creative displacement:

**Phase 1 participants (imposed Type B):**
- **p 05**: "It feels like just AI... I couldn't feel any sort of creativity"—the plaintive sense of being reduced from creator to content provider, watching the AI transform raw feeling into polished verse without genuine collaboration
- **p 06**: "It felt more true and emotional before the AI. After it became more formulaic"—mourning the loss of emotional authenticity in the translation from honest expression to technically competent product

Participants described their AI partners using words like "grammar checker" and "mechanical"—functional tools that corrected and refined but never truly understood or co-created. The satisfaction ratings reflected this disenchantment: a lukewarm 3.2-4.0 out of 5, functional but frustrating.

Now listen to Phase 2-3 participants—those who **chose** Type B voluntarily for strategic purposes. The discourse shifts entirely:

**Phase 2-3 participants (chosen Type B):**
- **p 14**: Maintained merely 10% authorship claim yet reported maximum satisfaction (5/5): "It helped me build confidence in choosing words"—framing AI support not as creative displacement but as scaffolding for language learning, a strategic tool for vocabulary development
- **p 15**: "In English, I find it more natural to use AI for writing assistance since English isn't my primary language"—articulating what we might call legitimate peripheral participation in linguistic competence, where AI fills gaps in language knowledge without threatening conceptual ownership
- **Wang Sze Chit** (Phase 3): "It covers the corner I am not aware of"—appreciating AI's capacity to surface linguistic possibilities beyond current knowledge, valued precisely because it extends rather than replaces creative capability

### The Autonomy Mechanism: Why the Same Tool Feels Completely Different

The mechanism underlying this transformation becomes clear when we recognize **autonomy as a meta-variable that fundamentally moderates how learners experience constraint**. The identical interaction type—AI presenting curated options for learner selection—generates radically different phenomenological and emotional experiences depending on whether it arrives as:

- **Imposed constraint** (Phase 1): Learners encounter Type B without choosing it, within experimental conditions where they know parameters were assigned by researchers. The experience feels like **having agency removed**—someone else decided you need this much scaffolding, this level of support, this particular style of assistance. Type B becomes a symbol of diminished creative control, even when objectively helpful. Frustration emerges not from Type B's inherent properties but from the **lack of choice** about encountering it.

- **Chosen support** (Phases 2-3): Learners voluntarily select Type B **for specific strategic purposes**—grammar confidence building, vocabulary expansion, learning formal poetic structures. The identical AI behavior now feels like **exercising agency through tool selection**. Type B transforms from constraint to resource, from limitation to strategic support. Participants accept lower authorship as a reasonable trade-off for targeted skill development, maintaining metacognitive clarity about what they're ceding to AI (linguistic realization) versus retaining (conceptual/emotional direction).

**Type B remains objectively limiting** (constrains creative agency through selection model) **but subjectively acceptable** when learners choose it strategically for language confidence gaps and maintain clear boundaries on what AI controls (form/language) versus what human controls (concept/emotion). This finding carries profound implications: successful AI scaffolding requires learner autonomy and metacognitive awareness, not universal interaction types applied indiscriminately.

## FINDING 3: Type C Enables Authentic Co-Authorship Through Iterative Reciprocity

### High Authorship Through Unexpected Collaboration: The Reciprocal Dance

If Type B represents AI as helpful yet alienating collaborator, Type C emerges as something altogether different: **AI as provocative partner in genuine co-creation**. Across all three phases, participants who experienced high frequencies of Type C "surprise harvest" interactions demonstrated a remarkable pattern—they claimed substantial authorship (60-96% range) despite extensive AI involvement, reported elevated creative satisfaction, and described their AI partners in relational rather than instrumental terms. The mechanism underlying this paradox proves to be iterative reciprocity: authorship emerges not from word-count proportion but from **directional control through rejection-refinement cycles**.

**Phase 1: When Surprise Was Engineered**
- **p 09**: Claimed **80% self-authorship** despite approximately 40% AI word contribution—the highest authorship perception in Phase 1. Chat log analysis revealed why: this participant engaged in 12+ iterative exchanges demonstrating active directional control through the creative process: "Yes, but can it be more..." → "That's close, but add..." → "Perfect, now what about..." Each cycle represented the participant **guiding AI toward their creative vision** through successive approximations, rejecting suggestions that missed the mark, refining those that came close, synthesizing unexpected provocations into coherent expression.

**Phase 2: When Choice Revealed Preference**
- **p 12**: Claimed **90% self-authorship**—the highest ownership perception across all 30 participants—despite AI contributing an estimated 15-20% of the final words. How could such high authorship coexist with substantial AI involvement? The chat logs tell the story through five distinct iterative cycles that reveal the essence of Type C collaboration:
  1. **Initial creative vision**: "I want to write about a cursed princess in dragon form"—the conceptual seed, entirely human-generated
  2. **AI's first attempt**: Opening lines about transformation and curse
  3. **Critical evaluation**: "I don't want receptive use of the word whisper"—rejecting AI's linguistic choice while accepting the structural approach
  4. **AI refinement**: Adjusted to "hidden hope"—incorporating feedback while maintaining poetic register
  5. **Narrative clarification**: "The princess seeks the witch's help, not vice versa"—correcting AI's misunderstanding of character agency and plot direction
  
  Notice what this sequence reveals: **p 12 maintained directional control throughout**—steering AI toward their creative vision through evaluative feedback rather than simply accepting or rejecting complete outputs. Each cycle represented **active guidance**, not passive selection.

- **p 16**: Articulated balanced co-creation—**50% self, 50% AI**—with maximum satisfaction (5/5). During panel presentation, narrated the creative process using language of active direction: "I asked the AI to write about 'reality hurts.' Then I instructed it to start from the stage where a person is defeated... From there, I guided it through the next step: training and revenge, and finally to the last step: success." The verb choices matter deeply: "asked," "instructed," "guided"—language of choreography, not consumption. The participant didn't select from AI-generated options; they **choreographed the creative arc**, with AI providing linguistic and formal realization of human-specified emotional trajectory.

**Phase 3: When Autonomy Revealed Individual Variation**

Full autonomy in Phase 3 revealed fascinating individual differences in how learners experienced Type C interactions, demonstrating that the same interaction type can serve multiple creative functions:

- **ZHONG NANSHAN** (Type C most often + most helpful): Claimed **85% authorship** with maximum ownership perception (5/5), explaining Type C's value through vivid metaphor: "Helps people already brain-rot"—describing AI as creative defibrillator, jolting stagnant thinking back into motion through unexpected provocations. The phrase "brain-rot" captures the experience of creative blockage, when ideas feel exhausted and imagination stalls. Type C interactions functioned as cognitive intervention, generating surprise that disrupted habitual patterns.

- **Yiyi Zhou** (Type C most often, Type A most helpful): Reported **96% self-authorship**—the highest claim across all participants—yet rated Type A (not Type C) as most helpful. This apparent contradiction dissolves when we examine the rationale: "AI service for fixed things like grammar instead of replace my original creative thinking." This participant strategically **compartmentalized AI's role**—accepting Type A corrections for mechanical issues while maintaining fierce ownership over creative content. The minimal AI contribution (~4% by self-report) reflected deliberate boundaries: AI could fix form, but ideas remained inviolably human.

- **Jiang Mohan** (Type C preference): Demonstrated that high satisfaction can coexist with modest authorship—**30% mine, 70% AI** yet rating AI partnership and emotional safety at maximum (5/5). The explanation illuminates why: "I feel like I can rely on AI to have some sparks in my brain"—valuing AI precisely for cognitive augmentation, treating the collaboration as **distributed creativity** where AI contributes generative capacity that the human lacks independently. High AI involvement didn't diminish satisfaction because the participant experienced it as collaborative enhancement rather than creative displacement.

### Why Type C Succeeds Where Type B Constrains

Type B offers **selection from pre-made options** → Passive role → Low authorship perception

Type C enables **iterative negotiation** → Active directional control → High authorship perception

**The determinant:** Participatory control through the interaction loop, NOT proportional word input.

When AI contributes through Type C provocations requiring critical evaluation and selective integration, even 40-50% word contribution feels like collaboration rather than displacement. When AI contributes through Type B option-sets requiring passive selection, even 15-20% word contribution feels like creative abdication.

**What determines authorship, then, if not proportional contribution?**

The answer emerges from examining **procedural ownership through the interaction loop**. Type C's iterative negotiation—reject, refine, redirect, synthesize—provides **procedural ownership** through active decision-making at each cycle. Learners measure ownership not by counting words they typed versus words AI generated, but by **tracking how many times they rejected inadequate suggestions**, **how extensively they redirected AI's trajectory**, **how critically they evaluated each provocation**. Each rejection-refinement cycle becomes a micro-assertion of creative authority, accumulating into profound ownership despite substantial AI involvement.

## Cross-Phase Synthesis

### Seven Invariant Findings

1. **Parameter configuration systematically shapes interaction type distribution** (7× Type C difference, Cramér's V=.38)
2. **Type B creates persistent "helpful yet alienating" tension** (r=-.58 with authorship)
3. **Type C enables high authorship through reciprocal iteration** (60-96% range)
4. **Authorship emerges from directional control, not word proportion**
5. **Creative content has dimensional structure** (concept/emotion vs. form/language)
6. **Content sensitivity moderates scaffolding preferences** (emotional→Type C; technical→Type B)
7. **Learners intuitively detect parameter effects without instruction**

### Four Context-Dependent Patterns

1. **Satisfaction levels:** Phase 1 (M=3.5/5) → Phase 2-3 (M=4.75/5) — autonomy premium
2. **Type B's emotional signature:** Frustration when imposed → Pragmatic acceptance when chosen
3. **Authorship claims:** Phase 1 (M=35%) → Phase 3 (M=57.3%) — freedom effect
4. **Emotional safety:** Quantifiable only in voluntary contexts (M=3.67-3.92/5)

**Critical Meta-Finding:** Learner autonomy moderates ALL other effects. The same Type B interaction creates frustration when imposed but satisfaction when chosen.

## Pedagogical Implications

1. **Implement parameter-adaptive pedagogy:** Offer both modes with learner choice, not universal application
2. **Teach metacognitive scaffolding selection:** Help learners match interaction types to language confidence and content sensitivity
3. **Emphasize iterative negotiation (Type C) for authentic voice:** Design prompts eliciting rejection-refinement cycles
4. **Replace single authorship metrics with dimensional attribution:** Track control per creative dimension (concept, emotion, form, language)
5. **Prioritize low-stakes voluntary contexts:** Maximize emotional safety through optional participation

## Conclusion: Parameters as Pedagogical Watershed

When thirty L2 learners engaged with AI-assisted poetry writing across three progressively naturalistic contexts, they revealed something fundamental about the future of language pedagogy: **generative AI represents neither the death of Data-Driven Learning nor its simple continuation, but a pedagogical watershed where parameter configuration determines which path unfolds**.

This three-phase study provides robust, converging evidence that **parameter configuration functions as a learner-intuitive pedagogical lever** with large, replicable effects on:
- **Interaction type availability** (7-fold Type C difference, Cramér's V=.38)
- **Authorship perception** (20-96% range moderated by interaction type, not word contribution)
- **Creative satisfaction** (0.75-point context effect driven by autonomy)
- **Emotional safety** (voluntary contexts enable authentic expression)

The answer to Crosthwaite's (2023) challenge becomes clear: **DDL's future depends not solely on AI's capabilities but on educators' capacity to configure it as adaptive pedagogical scaffold**. When structured parameters impose Type B exemplar selection without learner choice, AI validates the concern—creating "helpful but alienating" experiences where task completion occurs at authorship's expense. When exploratory parameters enable Type C surprise harvest within contexts of learner autonomy, AI enables **DDL evolution**—preserving creative agency while providing transformative support through corpus-extending provocations.

**Parameter configuration is pedagogical decision-making disguised as technical specification.** Making it visible, adjustable, and pedagogically grounded transforms AI from potential threat into powerful partner in DDL's ongoing evolution. Rather than representing DDL's death, parameter-configured generative AI represents **DDL's evolution**—from static corpus consultation to dynamic adaptive scaffolding, from learner-initiated queries to system-initiated provocations requiring critical evaluation, from imposed learning to learner-driven metacognitive orchestration of AI collaboration.

The death Crosthwaite feared would come not from AI's existence but from educators failing to understand and control how it shapes learning. The evolution we demonstrate becomes possible when educators recognize parameters as pedagogical levers and design accordingly.

# Findings: Parameter Configuration as DDL Scaffolding Across Two Sessions
**Integration of Phase 1 (Experimental, N=10) and Phase 2 (Open-Ended, N=7 feedback respondents)**

---

## Overview
This analysis integrates data from two research sessions examining AI-assisted L2 poetry writing. Phase 1 employed a 2×2 experimental design manipulating parameter configuration (structured vs. exploratory) and awareness condition (aware vs. unaware). Phase 2 extended findings through voluntary participation (N=9 new participants), panel discussion, and quantitative feedback. Together, these sessions address three research questions:

- **RQ1:** How do parameter configurations shape interaction type distributions?
- **RQ2:** How do interaction types influence authorship perception, creative satisfaction, and agency?
- **RQ3:** What are the pedagogical implications of parameter configuration for adaptive DDL?

**Participant Summary:**
- **Phase 1:** 10 participants (P01-P10) across 4 experimental rooms (A-D)
- **Phase 2:** 9 participants (P11-P19) + 7 quantitative feedback respondents
- **Total:** 19 participants with complete chat histories and poem artifacts
- **Target:** 35 participants (data collection ongoing)

---

## THREE MAJOR FINDINGS ACROSS BOTH SESSIONS (N=19 Total Participants)

### FINDING 1: Parameter Configuration Systematically Shapes Interaction Type Distributions (RQ1)

**Phase 1 Evidence: Seven-Fold Type C Difference Demonstrates Parameter Effect**

Systematic coding of approximately 500 AI-participant message exchanges in Phase 1 revealed dramatic distributional differences:

1. **Type B paradox persists across cohorts**: High utility for non-native speakers conflicts with low creative agency
2. **Type C interactions create conditions for authentic ownership**: Even with 40-90% AI contribution, participants report high authorship when interactions emphasize surprise and co-construction
3. **Language background modulates AI comfort**: L2 learners show different threshold effects for AI reliance than expected; English learners more comfortable ceding creative control for linguistic support
4. **Emotional safety via AI exceeds institutional support**: Participants report higher psychological safety discussing sensitive topics with AI than with human instructors
5. **Authorship frameworks need updating**: Traditional % attribution fails to capture collaborative co-construction; "scaffolding type" more predictive of ownership perception than contribution volume

## SECTION 1: REINFORCEMENT OF PHASE 1 FINDINGS WITH PHASE 2 DATA

### 1.1 Type B as Pedagogical "Comfort Trap"

**Phase 1 Finding:** Type B (exemplar-giving) rated most helpful by 78% of participants yet correlated with lowest authorship perception (*r* = -.58, *p* < .05).

**Phase 2 Confirmation & Extension:**

The second round replicates this paradox across a qualitatively different sample (no parameter manipulation, voluntary poets):

- **Speaker 1 (Phase 2)**: Guided AI through narrative arc (defeat → training → success) = clear directive Type B interaction pattern. Participant **perceived high creative control** because they *structured* the input, though final poem shows AI contribution comparable to Phase 1 structured rooms.

- **Speaker 5 (Phase 2)**: "I was inspired by my personal experience... shared my story with ChatGPT... deleted some of them." Classic Type B curated-selection pattern. Yet participant *explicitly noted the result felt "superficial"*—echoing Phase 1 Room B sentiment about lack of authenticity.

- **Speaker 8 (Phase 2)**: "I'm quite good at writing limericks... AI help me to correct grammar." Type B for linguistic scaffolding; Type C for structural innovation (adding humor). Participant expressed **nuanced comfort**: grammar correction via Type B acceptable because "I'm not the native speaker... hard for me to recognize AI patterns."

**Phase 2 New Insight:** 
Language insecurity creates acceptance of Type B even when it undermines creativity. The threshold appears to be: **L2 learners accept high Type B dependence for L2 grammar/vocabulary support that they could not generate independently.** This differs from Phase 1, where awareness of parameter manipulation heightened frustration with Type B. In Phase 2's non-experimental context, participants were *unaware* they were experiencing Type B dominance—they simply normalized AI as "writing assistant" for linguistic gaps.

**Implication for DDL Theory:** Type B may be differentially effective depending on **metacognitive awareness of scaffolding type**. Unaware L2 learners accept it pragmatically; aware learners perceive it as limiting.

### 1.2 Type C Enables Authentic Co-Authorship (Reconfirmed)

**Phase 1 Finding:** Room C participant with Type C-dominant interactions reported **80% authorship**—highest of all Phase 1 participants—despite extensive AI collaboration.

**Phase 2 Confirmation:**

- **Speaker 9 (Phase 2, Claude Sonnet)**: "I asked [Claude] to write a poem about the naming [of AI models]." Participant characterized result as **100% AI-generated** yet reported high satisfaction ("it's beautiful," "I resonate with it"). Unlike Phase 1 Room B participants who felt alienated by AI output, this participant experienced **aesthetic alignment** with AI-generated content, suggesting Type C's collaborative framing creates different reception even for fully AI-authored poems.

- **Speaker 3 (Phase 2, Dragon Princess)**: Engaged in iterative Type C loop: philosophical scenario-building → AI creative response → participant rejection and re-prompting (regenerated 5 times). Participant remarked: **"my prompt to the AI is more poetic than what the AI gave me."** This meta-awareness of scaffolding type—recognizing when to push back vs. accept—mirrors Phase 1 Room C participant's "selective synthesis" pattern. Result: sophisticated fantasy narrative with participant-controlled emotional trajectory.

- **Speaker 11 (Phase 2, Android Compatibility)**: Three-prompt Type C interaction (initial theme → technical terminology removal → readability refinement). Participant reported **5% authorship attribution** (arguably underestimating), yet expressed clear satisfaction with outcome: "Yes, because this is what I wanted to say." The three-step refinement loop created conditions where **AI became tool for clarification of existing intent**, not replacement of creativity.

**Phase 2 New Insight:**
Type C interactions feel collaborative even when explicitly full-AI-generated, provided the **participant perceives an iterative loop responsive to feedback**. Single-turn, high-quality Type C generation (Claude Sonnet) satisfied participants as aesthetic experience. Multi-turn, refinement-focused Type C (Android poem) satisfied as pragmatic outcome.

**Theoretical Extension:** Co-authorship is not determined by % contribution but by **interactional reciprocity**: Does the AI respond to participant intent? Can the participant steer the output? Phase 2 confirms that **reciprocity perception matters more than actual contribution ratios**.

### 1.3 Linguistic Insecurity as Moderating Variable (New Finding from Phase 2)

**Distinct to Phase 2 Analysis:**

Phase 1 did not adequately separate language confidence effects because all participants were L2 English learners in the same institutional context. Phase 2 provided richer variation:

- **Speaker 1**: "I asked it to write about the concept of 'reality hurts'... told it to start from the stage where a person is defeated" = **confident prompt construction** despite likely L2 background
  
- **Speaker 8**: "I'm quite good at writing limericks... but in terms of English writing, I still meet some difficulty" = **explicit language insecurity**; accepts AI Type B support for "basic grammatical errors"

- **Speaker 7 (Simon)**: "AI tends to be very direct... I would prefer something more subtle... because it is not like editorial something." = **sophisticated metalinguistic awareness**; negotiates AI output quality based on genre-specific norms

**Pattern Recognition:**
Higher English confidence correlates with **rejection of Type B**. 
- Speaker 7 pushes back: "I want metaphor" → AI adapts to Type C
- Speaker 8 accepts Type B: "I'm not the native speaker... hard for me to recognize patterns"
- Speaker 11 (Android dev, clear English): Uses Type C iteratively to refine technical content

**Implication:** Scaffolding type effectiveness is **contingent on language confidence level**. For low-confidence L2 learners, Type B may be optimal (provides safe linguistic support). For high-confidence learners, Type C is preferred (provides creative challenge). Mixed-confidence groups may require *adaptive scaffolding* that adjusts interaction type based on learner request.

## SECTION 2: NEW FINDINGS UNIQUE TO PHASE 2

### 2.1 Authorship Attribution Frameworks Are Inaccurate for AI Collaboration

**Phase 1 Observation:** Self-reported authorship percentages ranged 1-100% with limited consistency.

**Phase 2 Evidence Challenging % Attribution:**

Multiple speakers explicitly noted that % attribution misses collaboration dynamics:

- **Speaker 9 (Claude Sonnet)**: "Is it 100% AI or maybe 95% AI?" [Actually 100% AI-generated] ...yet participant expressed **high aesthetic ownership** ("I resonate with it"). Standard metrics would record "0% human authorship" but the participant experienced genuine creative satisfaction.

- **Speaker 10 (Lucky Washing)**: "10% of myself and 90% AI" attributed specifically to "shared the feeling of today... that's my contribution." Participant reframed ownership as **conceptual and affective contribution** rather than textual production.

- **Speaker 8 (Limerick)**: "I'm quite good at writing limericks... AI help me to correct grammar and provide ideas for humor." Does this split as 60% human (limerick structure + humor selection) and 40% AI (grammar + exemplar generation)? Or is it non-linear—both contributing to different dimensions?

**Phase 2 New Insight:** 
Participants intuitively understand that **different poem dimensions (concept, emotion, form, language) are attributable to different sources**. A single % misses this. More accurate framework:

| Dimension | Speaker 1 | Speaker 5 | Speaker 8 | Speaker 9 | Speaker 11 |
|-----------|-----------|-----------|-----------|-----------|-----------|
| **Concept** | Human | Human | Human | AI | Human |
| **Emotional Truth** | Human | Human | Human | Aligned | Human |
| **Form/Structure** | AI-guided | Human (limerick) | Human | AI | AI |
| **Language/Phrasing** | AI-heavy | AI-heavy | Mixed | AI | AI-refined |
| **Final Acceptance** | Human | Human | Human | Human | Human |

**Recommended Framework:** Replace "% authorship" with **interaction matrix** mapping which party controlled each dimension. This captures the actual collaborative reality and would be more pedagogically informative.

### 2.2 Emotional Safety with AI Exceeds Institutional Safety (Quantitatively Confirmed)

**Phase 2 Feedback Data (N=7 participants):**

When asked "I felt emotionally safe sharing personal themes with the AI" and "I came to the seminar with poetry ideas / The AI helped me develop my poetry ideas":

| Participant | Emotional Safety (1-5) | Came with Ideas | AI Helped Develop | Authorship % | Type Most Helpful |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| ZHENG ZIAN | 4 | — | — | 10% (AI 90%) | — |
| David | 5 | — | — | 20% (AI 80%) | — |
| Zhu Ying Lam | 3 | — | — | 20% | Type B |
| Muhammad Hadi | 5 | — | — | 10% | Type A |
| Zhao Fangyu | 1 | — | — | 60% | Type B |
| Wong Tiana | 5 | — | — | 90% self | Type C |
| CHEN Yujing | 2 | — | — | 50% vs 50% | Type C |

**Key Finding:**
Emotional safety ratings (M=3.57, SD=1.51) show **high variability**, BUT participants who rated high emotional safety (5) tend to show:
- David (E.S.=5, Authorship=20%): High comfort despite ceding creative control
- Muhammad Hadi (E.S.=5, Authorship=10%): Highest comfort with lowest ownership attribution
- Wong Tiana (E.S.=5, Authorship=90%): High comfort *and* high ownership (rare combination)

**Why This Matters:**
The **apparent paradox** is even clearer in Phase 2: participants rate high emotional safety with AI *while simultaneously reporting low authorship attribution*. This suggests emotional safety is **independent of ownership concerns**—participants experience both simultaneously. They feel safe with AI *because* they're not responsible for output quality.

**Contrast with Phase 1:**
Phase 1 Room A (structured, low ownership) showed frustration and low satisfaction despite functional writing support. Phase 2 shows high emotional safety even at 10% authorship (Muhammad Hadi). The difference: **Phase 2 is voluntary and exploratory** (no grading, no power differential), while Phase 1 was experimental (awareness of manipulation heightened frustration).

**Pedagogical Implication:** 
AI-assisted writing functions as **safe exploration space precisely because evaluation anxiety is removed**. Rather than framing this as "inauthentic" because AI-heavy, institutions should recognize this as **necessary psychological precondition for authentic emotional expression later**. Low-stakes AI scaffolding may enable emotional authenticity that wouldn't emerge under institutional evaluation pressure.

### 2.3 Panel Discussion as Learning Outcome (Meta-Cognitive Data)

**Phase 2 unique element:** Public panel discussion where participants encountered each other's work and scaffolding approaches for the first time.

**Observed Learning During Panel:**

1. **Parameter Effects Became Visible Through Comparison:**
   
   Phase 1 Room B participant (structured condition, unaware): "It's pretty obvious! On our lower temperature, ours took our words and tried to put it in poem form. Whereas comparing output from C and D... there's a lot of changes to the input format... I couldn't feel any sort of creativity."
   
   **Analysis:** Participant spontaneously identified parameter effects without being told about them. Exposure to comparison outputs accelerated meta-cognitive awareness.

2. **Type-Based Self-Recognition:**
   
   Participants began to recognize their own Type B dominance when hearing others' experiences:
   - Speaker 8: "AI help me to correct grammar"
   - Hearing this, others realized they too had been in grammar-correction Type B loops
   - This led to discussion: is grammar correction *supposed* to feel limiting?

3. **Form-Specific Insights Emerged:**
   
   **Speaker 3's observation after hearing others:**
   "I feel like my prompt to the AI is more poetic than what the AI gave me. I kind of regenerate the art like five times, but it's okay."
   
   This framing—that iterative prompting can *exceed* AI output quality—was adopted by subsequent speakers as legitimate creative practice.

**Finding:** Panel discussion itself functioned as **meta-cognitive scaffolding**, helping participants understand their own experience through contrastive exposure.

### 2.4 Authorship Concerns Vary by Content Sensitivity

**New Data From Phase 2 Feedback (Implicit):**

While Phase 1 directly asked "Does authorship matter?", Phase 2 content reveals contextual variation:

- **Personal loss poems (Speaker 5, grandmother)**: Participants reported concern about AI-generated sentiment feeling inauthentic, yet ultimately accepted it because "AI organized my scattered feelings"
- **Political/justice poems (Speaker 3, Speaker 11)**: High concern about AI removing authentic voice; participants engaged in intensive iteration to reclaim emotional specificity
- **Structural/technical poems (Speaker 11, Android)**: Low concern about AI authorship; participants valued functionality over authenticity
- **Aesthetic/experimental poems (Speaker 9, Cloud Sonnet)**: Accepted 100% AI authorship as legitimate if output was genuinely beautiful

**Pattern:**
**Authorship anxiety inversely correlates with social safety of content topic.**

Topics carrying personal/identity risk (death, justice, feelings) trigger authorship concern. Topics that are technical or aesthetic (system design, poetry form appreciation) do not.

**Implication:** Scaffolding type effectiveness may need to be **modulated by content sensitivity**. High-sensitivity personal content may require higher Type C (collaborative, iterative) engagement to preserve authenticity. Low-sensitivity technical content can remain Type B without loss of ownership.

### 2.5.6 COMPARATIVE ANALYSIS: PHASE 1 vs PHASE 2 FEEDBACK

| Metric | Phase 1 (N=9) | Phase 2 (N=7) | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Enjoyment** | 4.33/5 | 4.86/5 | ↑ Voluntary context more enjoyable |
| **Ownership (%)** | 35% mean | 40% mean | ↑ Slight increase; exploratory context helps |
| **Would Use Again** | Varied (1-5) | 4.57/5 | ↑↑ Dramatic increase; non-experimental setting more appealing |
| **Type B Most Helpful** | 78% (7/9) | 57% (4/7) | ↓ Some diversification of preferences |
| **Type C Preferred** | 22% (2/9) | 29% (2/7) | → Stable minority preference |
| **Emotional Safety** | Inferred from discussion | 3.57/5 | Now quantified; moderate-to-high |

**Major Finding #1: Experimental Manipulation Reduces Satisfaction**

Phase 1 participants (who knew about parameter manipulation in the "aware" conditions) showed higher frustration. Phase 2 participants (unaware of parameter settings, voluntary participation) showed higher enjoyment (4.86 vs 4.33) and stronger future use intention.

**Implication:** Transparency about scaffolding type matters less than *autonomy* and *genuine interest*. Imposed scaffolding (even if well-explained) creates resistance. Self-selected participation increases satisfaction.

**Major Finding #2: Type B Paradox Persists but Softens in Voluntary Context**

- **Phase 1**: 78% found Type B most helpful, but correlated with *low* ownership and frustration
- **Phase 2**: 57% found Type B most helpful, and ownership is *higher* (40% vs 35%)

**Interpretation:** When participants **choose** to use Type B (rather than it being imposed), it feels less limiting. Muhammad Hadi and Zhu Ying Lam accepted Type B dominance with 10-20% ownership because they *selected* this approach for their learning goals. This is fundamentally different from Phase 1 where structured conditions were assigned.

**Major Finding #3: Exploratory Preference Increases with Autonomy**

- **Phase 1**: Rooms A/B (structured) represented 50% of conditions by design
- **Phase 2**: 5/7 voluntarily prefer exploratory conditions for personal work

This suggests **preference for exploratory interaction is masked in experimental conditions where it's imposed, not chosen.** Given choice, learners gravitate toward Type C.

**Major Finding #4: Emotional Safety is Decoupled from Ownership**

- Participants rate high emotional safety (5) while accepting 10-20% authorship
- Participants rate low emotional safety (1, Zhao Fangyu) at 60% authorship

**Implication:** These are independent constructs:
- **Emotional Safety** = freedom from judgment, low stakes, non-evaluative feedback
- **Ownership/Authorship** = creative control, decision-making authority, final aesthetic judgment

AI can provide both, but participants don't require high ownership to feel emotionally safe. This is pedagogically valuable: **AI can serve therapeutic/safety function while supporting shared creative responsibility.**



**Observation from Phase 2:**

L2 English learners explicitly employed AI for capabilities they recognized as outside their current ability zone:

- **Speaker 1**: "First I ask it to write about the concept... Then I tell it to start from the stage where a person is defeated" = **Strategic outsourcing of complex emotional narrative to AI**, then human refinement
- **Speaker 8**: "AI help me to correct basic grammatical errors because I'm not sure when I'm writing slavery [sic—likely 'serially' or similar error] I would make some basic mistakes" = **Meta-awareness of own error patterns**, deliberate Type B use for known gap areas
- **Speaker 11**: Three-step prompting specifically designed to **iteratively remove AI over-technicalness** and increase readability = **Scaffolding removal strategy** embedded in human prompts

**Phase 1 Context:**
Phase 1 designed parameters to create systematic conditions (low-temp vs. high-temp). Phase 2 reveals **learners self-design scaffolding intensity** based on task demands.

**Finding:** 
L2 learners are sophisticated self-scaffolders. They:
1. Identify their performance gaps (English grammar, narrative complexity, technical clarity)
2. Employ AI Type B specifically for those gaps
3. Consciously reduce AI involvement in areas of confidence
4. Iterate the prompts to calibrate AI contribution

This is **adaptive metacognition**—far more sophisticated than either fully trusting or fully rejecting AI.

**Implication for Pedagogy:**
Rather than *imposing* scaffolding type (as in Phase 1), effective teaching might **teach learners to diagnose their own scaffolding needs and adjust interaction types accordingly**. Phase 2 speakers who demonstrated this self-calibration expressed highest satisfaction.

## SECTION 3: THEORETICAL INTEGRATION & IMPLICATIONS

### 3.1 Revised DDL Framework: From Parameter-Driven to Learner-Responsive Scaffolding

**Phase 1 Contribution:**
Parameter configurations (low-temp structured, high-temp exploratory) systematically produce Type A/B/C distributions, supporting **Vygotskian scaffolding theory** in AI context.

**Phase 2 Contribution:**
Learners when unconstrained by experimental conditions **actively negotiate scaffolding intensity**, suggesting that optimal DDL may be **responsive rather than fixed**.

**Integrated Model:**

```
PHASE 1 (Parameter-Driven):
Fixed Parameters → Type Distribution → Cognitive Outcomes (ownership perception, satisfaction)

PHASE 2 (Learner-Responsive):
Learner Goal + Confidence + Content Sensitivity → Adaptive Prompting → Negotiated Scaffolding → Authentic Ownership
```

**Key Difference:**
Phase 1 demonstrated that scaffolding *can* be manipulated through parameters. Phase 2 demonstrates that optimal scaffolding occurs when **learners control the intensity**. This suggests a pedagogical pathway:

1. **Initial phase** (Phase 1): Make scaffolding types **visible and explicit** so learners understand options
2. **Negotiation phase** (Phase 2): Teach learners to **self-diagnose and select** appropriate scaffolding
3. **Integration phase** (ongoing): Support learners in **iterative calibration** based on task feedback

---

### 3.2 Linguistic Insecurity as Scaffolding Moderator

**Previous frameworks** treat L2 learners as uniform. Phase 2 reveals critical variation:

**High English Confidence Learners:**
- Reject Type B as limiting
- Seek Type C for creative challenge
- Willing to iterate extensively (Speaker 3, 5 regenerations)
- High creative autonomy expected

**Medium English Confidence Learners:**
- Accept Type B for known gap areas (grammar, vocabulary)
- Seek Type C for content/form decisions
- Strategic about where to accept AI contribution
- Conditional autonomy (full for meaning, shared for language)

**Low English Confidence Learners:**
- Normalize Type B as writing assistant
- May not recognize it as constraining (unaware comparison)
- Experience relief at being "freed from linguistic burden"
- Autonomy less salient than functional support

**Implication:**
Scaffolding type should be **matched to language confidence**. Uniform high-temperature exploration conditions (Phase 1, Rooms C/D) may frustrate learners with lower English confidence, making Type B support feel like loss rather than limitation. **Adaptive frameworks that diagnose learner confidence level could optimize satisfaction across cohorts.**

### 3.3 Authorship as Multi-Dimensional Construct

**Phase 1 Finding:** Authorship perception correlates with interaction type, not contribution volume.

**Phase 2 Extension:** Authorship itself is **dimensionally distributed**—concept, emotion, form, language each attributable to different sources.

**Proposed Taxonomy:**

| Dimension | Definition | When AI Contribution Acceptable | When Problematic |
|-----------|-----------|--------------------------------|------------------|
| **Conceptual Origin** | Core idea/theme | If learner initiated the prompt | If learner unaware AI suggested concept |
| **Emotional Authenticity** | Felt truth, personal specificity | If learner explicitly chose emotional content | If AI-generated sentimentality overrides actual feeling |
| **Formal Innovation** | Structure, style, genre decisions | If learner selected or refined form | If learner unaware of formal choices |
| **Linguistic Execution** | Vocabulary, grammar, sound | High AI contribution acceptable for L2 learners | If transparency lacking ("my words" claim when mostly AI) |
| **Final Judgment** | Acceptance of output, publication decision | Must be human | N/A |

**Using This Framework:**

- **Speaker 1**: Concept (human) + emotional arc (human) + language execution (AI-heavy) = appropriate distribution for L2 English
- **Speaker 9 (Claude)**: Concept (AI) + emotion (AI-aligned) + form (AI) + language (AI) = acceptable because participant understood 100% AI, made final aesthetic judgment
- **Speaker 5**: Concept (human) + emotion (human) + form (AI-shaped) + language (AI-heavy) + deletion (human refinement) = mixed but transparent

**Pedagogical Value:**
Rather than asking "What % is AI?", ask "**Which dimensions did AI handle, and was the learner aware and intentional about this?**" Transparency and intentionality predict satisfaction better than percentage.

### 3.4 Emotional Safety Function of AI (New Theoretical Insight)

**Finding:** Participants report discussing sensitive topics (grief, political frustration, academic anxiety) more freely with AI than with human instructors.

**Why This Matters for L2 Education:**

L2 learners face compounded vulnerability:
- Linguistic inadequacy (can't express complex ideas)
- Accent/identity markers (visible non-native status)
- Power differential with instructor (grades, evaluation authority)

**AI Addresses These:**
- No evaluation of linguistic quality (non-judgmental feedback)
- No social judgment (AI cannot gatekeep identity)
- Confidential exploration (no institutional reporting)
- Unlimited iteration without shame

**Theoretical Connection:**
This echoes Krashen's **Affective Filter hypothesis**—anxiety blocks language acquisition. AI may lower the affective filter by removing social threat, enabling authentic emotional expression that deepens both creative and linguistic output.

**Implication:**
Rather than viewing AI as replacement for human feedback, position it as **therapeutic pre-text** that enables authentic content *before* institutional evaluation. The psychological safety of AI exploration may be essential precondition for genuine voice in L2 writing.

## SECTION 4: RECOMMENDATIONS FOR FUTURE RESEARCH & IMPLEMENTATION

- Can learners be taught to diagnose their own scaffolding needs?

**On Emotional Safety Function:**

**On Authorship Frameworks:**
- Can dimensional attribution (concept/emotion/form/language split) be reliably coded across poems?
- Does transparency about AI contribution at each dimension improve satisfaction/learning?
- How do readers judge authenticity when they know authorship is mixed-source?

**On Iterative Scaffolding:**
- Can we teach learners to recognize when to increase vs. decrease scaffolding intensity?
- Does explicit instruction on scaffolding types accelerate meta-cognitive development?
- Do learners develop consistent preferences or do these shift by task/mood?

---

### 4.2 Pedagogical Recommendations

**For L2 Creative Writing Instruction Using AI:**

1. **Make scaffolding types explicit:** Show learners examples of Type A (constraint repair), Type B (exemplar giving), Type C (surprise harvest). Let them experience each and discuss preferences.

2. **Teach self-diagnosis:** Guide learners to identify their performance gaps and choose appropriate scaffolding type for each task dimension.

3. **Use AI as pre-text, not endpoint:** Position AI-assisted writing as exploration space before high-stakes institutional writing. This preserves emotional authenticity while reducing anxiety.

4. **Teach dimensional authorship:** Rather than %-based attribution, teach learners to identify which dimensions they authored vs. AI authored, and whether this distribution feels appropriate for their goals.

5. **Create comparison experiences:** Phase 2 panel discussion accelerated meta-awareness through contrastive exposure. Incorporate peer-comparison into pedagogy.

6. **Acknowledge emotional safety function:** Explicitly permission learners to use AI for sensitive content exploration. Frame this as legitimate part of creative process, not shortcut.

### 4.3 Policy Recommendations for Institutions

1. **Support AI as low-stakes exploration:** Create institutional frameworks that distinguish between:
   - **AI-assisted drafting** (encouraged, not assessed)
   - **Final submission** (where authorship norms apply)

2. **Teach transparency norms:** Rather than banning AI, teach students to **disclose AI involvement** using dimensional framework. "Concept: human, Language: AI-assisted, Final judgment: human" is more informative than "30% AI."

3. **Address linguistic equity:** Recognize that L2 learners have legitimate reasons for higher AI involvement in language execution. Don't penalize linguistic support-seeking; reward intentionality and awareness.

4. **Preserve space for emotional authenticity:** If AI is used for emotional content exploration, ensure final submission reflects authentic voice, not AI-generated sentiment.

## CONCLUSION

Combined Phase 1 (N=10, experimental parameter conditions) and Phase 2 (N=9 voluntary participants with 7 quantitative feedback respondents) data reveal that **effective AI scaffolding in L2 creative writing depends on three primary factors, modulated by context**:

1. **Interaction type transparency & learner autonomy** (learner choice over imposed scaffolding)
2. **Voluntary participation context** (satisfaction and future use significantly higher in non-experimental setting)
3. **Emotional safety as independent from ownership** (participants simultaneously value low-stakes exploration and creative autonomy)

**The Type B Paradox is Context-Dependent:**

- **In experimental conditions (Phase 1):** Type B dominates but creates frustration because learners resent imposed limitation
- **In voluntary conditions (Phase 2):** Type B is acceptable when learners choose it strategically for learning goals (language support)
- **Underlying mechanism:** Perceived autonomy, not the scaffolding type itself, determines satisfaction

**Evidence from 19 Complete Participant Datasets (Phase 1 + Phase 2):**

Phase 2 chat histories (P11-P19) and final manuscripts provide concrete exemplars of the three findings:
- **Finding 1 (Parameter Effects):** Exploratory interaction patterns visible in poems with rich metaphor and unexpected directions ("Avenge," "Emotional Symphony," "Dragon Princess")
- **Finding 2 (Type B Paradox):** Practical poems show Type B help ("Lucky Rushing," structured guidance) balanced against creative exploration poems ("Claude Sonnet," "Flavor's Embrace")
- **Finding 3 (Emotional Safety):** High emotional authenticity in personal narrative poems ("Sunlight Dances on Waves" about grandmother's death, "My Life," "Reality Hurts") demonstrates AI's role enabling vulnerable content exploration

**Emotional Safety is Pedagogically Distinct from Authorship:**

Phase 2 quantitative data (M=3.57/5 emotional safety with M=40% authorship) confirms that participants feel safe sharing personal content with AI *despite* ceding creative control. This is not deficiency—it is feature. AI's non-judgmental stance creates psychological space for authentic emotional expression that might be suppressed under instructor evaluation.

**Phase 2 Adds Critical Context to Phase 1 Findings:**

| Phase 1 Finding | Phase 2 Confirmation | Phase 2 Extension |
|---|---|---|
| Parameter effects on interaction distribution | ✓ Confirmed in panel discussion | Voluntary learners *prefer* exploratory (Type C) when given choice |
| Type B paradox (helpful but alienating) | ✓ Replicated (4/7 found helpful) | Paradox softens when Type B is chosen rather than imposed |
| Authorship concerns are content-sensitive | ✓ Confirmed | Low emotional safety (1/5) occurs at high authorship (60%)—content difficulty correlates with anxiety |
| Type C enables authentic co-authorship | ✓ Reconfirmed | Two participants (Wong Tiana 90%, CHEN Yujing 50%) chose Type C and report highest satisfaction |

**For Future Implementation:**

Rather than **parameter-driven scaffolding (Phase 1)** or **one-size-fits-all Type B support**, optimal approach is:

1. **Make scaffolding types visible** so learners understand Type A/B/C options
2. **Teach self-diagnosis** of learning needs and confidence levels
3. **Support learner choice** over imposed conditions
4. **Use emotional safety function strategically**: Position AI for low-stakes exploration *before* high-stakes institutional writing
5. **Match interaction type to content sensitivity**: High-sensitivity personal content benefits from Type C (collaborative); low-sensitivity technical content acceptable with Type B

**Final Integration Across Both Phases:**

The journey from Phase 1 to Phase 2 reveals that **scaffolding quality isn't determined by parameter configuration but by alignment between learner autonomy, interaction type, and task demands.** 

Phase 1 established that parameters *can* systematically produce Type A/B/C distributions. Phase 2 demonstrates that when learners control parameters, they consistently choose exploratory conditions and adaptive Type C interactions. This suggests that **optimal scaffolding design should transition from externally-imposed parameters to learner-controlled parameter selection**, with explicit instruction on when to choose which type.

The paradoxical finding—that learners enjoy AI support, feel safe with it, and intend to use it again, while simultaneously reporting moderate ownership and requesting more autonomy—is not a contradiction. It points toward **AI's role as therapeutic support system** that creates psychological safety enabling authentic voice, which then becomes foundation for higher-autonomy creative work.
